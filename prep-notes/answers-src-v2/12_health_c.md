## A1.7 Claims lifecycle and payment integrity strategy (additional set)

### Q1.7.1 [Conceptual] Walk me through the end-to-end U.S. healthcare claims lifecycle. Where would you insert pre-submission, pre-pay, and post-pay payment-integrity capabilities?
*Source: Top 70 document, question 6.*
I think about the claims lifecycle as a sequence of decisions rather than just a transaction moving from provider to payer. It starts before the claim even exists. A patient receives care, the provider captures documentation, diagnoses and procedures are coded, eligibility and benefits are checked, and in some cases prior authorization is obtained. Then the provider creates the claim, typically sends it electronically through an 837 transaction, and the claim goes through validation before entering the payer's adjudication process. During adjudication, the payer determines whether the member was eligible, whether the service is covered, whether the coding is valid, what the provider contract says, whether another insurer should pay first, and what the allowed amount and member responsibility should be. Once adjudicated, payment and remittance information flow back to the provider, commonly through the 835 ERA, which includes adjustment reasons and financial responsibility. After that you still have corrections, appeals, adjustments and potentially post-payment audits or recoveries. Where payment integrity becomes interesting is that I would not treat it as one checkpoint. I would put controls at three levels. Pre-submission, I want to prevent predictable errors while the provider is still creating the claim. That could be coding guidance, eligibility and COB validation, or real-time edits. Optum is explicitly moving capabilities in this direction because fixing something before submission avoids a denial altogether. Pre-pay, once the claim reaches the payer, I would apply deterministic edits, pricing and contract rules, fraud signals, predictive scoring and targeted clinical review before money leaves the system. Optum's portfolio currently places claim pricing, editing, fraud detection and claim review in this part of the lifecycle. Post-pay, I still need data mining, contract audits, COB correction and recovery because no pre-pay system will catch everything, and post-pay outcomes are also valuable training data for the upstream controls. The product opportunity is therefore not simply to build better audits. It is to create a closed loop where every valid post-pay finding makes the pre-pay system smarter, and every recurring pre-pay issue becomes something we eventually help the provider correct before submission. That is how I would think about moving from payment integrity as a set of controls to payment accuracy across the entire claims lifecycle.

### Q1.7.2 [Conceptual] What is payment integrity, and how would you evolve a traditional post-pay audit-and-recovery program toward prevention-first "payment precision"?
*Source: Top 70 document, question 7.*
I would describe payment integrity very simply as making sure the right claim is paid to the right provider, for the right service, at the right amount, under the right coverage and contract terms. Optum defines it similarly, covering eligibility, coding and billing, benefits, pricing, fraud, waste and abuse across pre-submission, pre-pay and post-pay activities. If I inherited a traditional post-pay program, I would first resist the temptation to immediately move everything upstream. Post-pay is actually where you have some of your best evidence. You know which errors were real, which providers or claim types produced them, how much was recovered, how long the investigation took, how often the provider challenged the finding and whether it was overturned. I would use that history to create an error taxonomy and rank opportunities by frequency, financial impact, confidence and preventability. Then I would move the highest-confidence, repeatable issues upstream in stages. If it is a deterministic coding rule, it may become a pre-pay edit. If the issue comes from contract interpretation, I might digitize the contract terms and use them during adjudication. If the same provider repeatedly makes a correctable billing mistake, the better answer may be pre-submission guidance rather than repeatedly denying the claim. Optum describes this evolution as moving from post-payment correction to pre-pay intervention and now toward what it calls 'payment precision,' where real-time information helps prevent the error before submission. I like that model because it changes the objective. I am no longer celebrating how much money I recovered after an incorrect payment. I am trying to make the incorrect payment never happen. I would still retain post-pay capabilities because there will always be novel errors, complex cases and fraud patterns that cannot be completely predicted. But I would turn post-pay into the learning engine for the rest of the platform. Over time, I would want to see the mix of savings shift upstream, recovery cost fall, cycle time improve, appeals reduce and provider friction come down. In my own platform work, I have seen the value of that kind of progression. When recurring implementation and operational issues were converted into reusable product capabilities rather than solved repeatedly, we reduced implementation time by 75%. I would apply the same product instinct here: use what you learn from repeated exceptions to redesign the system so fewer exceptions happen in the first place.

### Q1.7.3 [Technical] Assume audit yield has fallen 15% and cycle time has increased 20%, while gross identified savings have increased. How would you diagnose the situation?
*Source: Top 70 document, question 8.*
**Clarifying questions:** Which audit types and lines of business are affected? Did volume, mix or thresholds change in the same period? How is "yield" defined (valid findings per review, or upheld dollars per review)? Are overturn and appeal data available by concept?

**Assumptions (illustrative numbers):** a pre-change month had 10,000 reviews, 20 percent yield (2,000 findings), $400 average finding, 8 percent overturn and $45 cost per review. The current month has 12,500 reviews, 17 percent yield (15 percent lower in relative terms), $480 average finding, 18 percent overturn and cycle time up 20 percent.

**Framework:** a funnel decomposition (screened, selected, reviewed, valid, upheld, realized) plus a net-value check, then an issue tree of causes (selection, review process, rules or models, mix, data).

**Worked estimate:**
- Before: 2,000 findings × $400 = $800,000 gross; × (1 − 0.08) = $736,000 upheld; minus 10,000 × $45 = $450,000 review cost, so net about $286,000.
- After: 12,500 × 17 percent = 2,125 findings × $480 = $1,020,000 gross (up 27.5 percent); × (1 − 0.18) = $836,400 upheld; minus 12,500 × $45 = $562,500, so net about $273,900.
- *Sanity check:* gross savings rose 27.5 percent but net value fell about 4 percent, which is exactly the pattern the question describes.

**Answer (spoken):**
My first reaction would be that the headline savings number may be hiding deterioration in the underlying system. If audit yield is down 15%, we are reviewing more claims that do not result in valid findings. If cycle time is also up 20%, each review is taking longer. So even though gross identified savings have increased, I would want to know whether we are actually creating more net value, or whether a small number of very large claims are making the topline look healthy while productivity and precision are declining. I would start by decomposing the metric. I would segment audit yield and cycle time by audit type, claim type, line of business, provider, dollar band, reviewer type, rule or model, and whether medical records were required. I would also compare identified savings with validated savings, realized savings, appeals and overturns. That last part matters because a very aggressive selection model can increase identified dollars while simultaneously generating more false positives and provider disputes. Optum's own claim-review approach uses predictive scoring to target claims with a high likelihood of error and then routes appropriate cases to certified coders or clinicians, so selection quality is a major lever. I would then look for what changed. Did we recently expand into more complex reimbursement methodologies? Did thresholds change? Did a model drift? Are reviewers waiting longer for medical records? Did new contract logic create false positives? Has the provider mix shifted? Are high-value claims being over-selected because the model optimizes dollar opportunity rather than probability of a valid finding? From a product perspective, I would create a simple funnel: claims screened, claims selected, claims reviewed, valid findings, findings upheld after appeal, dollars prevented or recovered, and total operational cost. That lets me calculate something closer to expected net value instead of gross opportunity. I have used the same diagnostic approach in other enterprise platforms. When a performance metric looked healthy at the aggregate level, breaking it down by workflow and bottleneck exposed where users were actually losing time, and that led to changes that reduced execution time by as much as 70%. Here, I would follow the same principle: don't optimize the number that looks best on the dashboard. Find where the system is losing precision and time, and fix the part of the funnel creating the deterioration.
**Recommendation:** treat the gross savings number as a vanity metric until proven otherwise. Report net upheld value, yield and cycle time together, find which concepts or models drive the false positives, tighten or pause them, and set guardrails (a maximum overturn rate and cycle time) before expanding volume.

### Q1.7.4 [Conceptual] How would you increase medical-cost savings without increasing provider abrasion, appeals, false positives, or administrative burden?
*Source: Top 70 document, question 9.*
I would treat that as an optimization problem with several constraints, not as 'maximize savings.' If the team only optimizes savings, the easiest way to increase it is to flag more claims, ask for more records and deny more aggressively. That may work on one dashboard, but it creates appeals, overturns, administrative cost and provider frustration. Optum itself recognizes that payment-integrity interventions are a major source of provider rework, and its current strategy is explicitly about improving payment accuracy while reducing provider abrasion. So my first lever would be better selection precision. I would prioritize claims using expected value, not just claim size. Something like probability that the finding is valid multiplied by financial opportunity, then adjusted for review cost and provider-friction risk. Claims with high confidence and deterministic logic can be automated. Ambiguous claims should go to specialists rather than be treated like obvious errors. The second lever is to move predictable issues upstream. If a provider routinely submits the same coding error, the highest-value intervention is probably not the twentieth denial. It is giving that provider the rule or correction before submission. Optum's Cost Avoidance products are designed around that idea. Third, I would improve the evidence presented with every intervention. A provider should be able to understand exactly why an adjustment occurred, which policy, coding guideline or contract term applies, and what evidence supports the decision. Contract intelligence is interesting here because Optum reports that digitizing contract terms can reduce false positives and administrative overhead while potentially improving medical-cost savings. Fourth, I would measure appeals and overturns by edit or audit concept. A concept with high savings but also a high overturn rate should trigger a product review. Finally, I would create a feedback loop with providers and Operations. One of the lessons I've learned from enterprise SaaS is that the best efficiency gains often come from removing the reason users need support, not from making the support team faster. In one platform, turning recurring complexity into configurable workflows reduced customer onboarding time by 75%. I would bring the same mindset here. The best payment-integrity outcome is not a successful dispute. It is a correct claim that required no dispute at all.

### Q1.7.5 [Technical] Design a configurable payment-integrity workflow covering intake, claim selection, audit execution, evidence/documentation, decisioning, appeals, recovery, and reporting.
*Source: Top 70 document, question 10.*
**Clarifying questions:** Which audit types are in scope (professional, facility, contract compliance, post-pay)? Who configures workflow (clients or internal teams)? What integrations exist with adjudication and recovery systems? What evidence and audit trail requirements apply?

**Assumptions:** a multi-client payer-facing platform, several lines of business, pre-pay and post-pay audits on a common core, client administrators who configure rules and workflow within guardrails.

**Framework:** R-C-A-D-T (requirements, capacity, APIs and data, design, trade-offs), organized along the audit lifecycle (intake, selection, work orchestration, evidence, decision, appeal, recovery, reporting).

**Answer (spoken):**
I would design this as one common platform with configurable workflow components rather than separate applications for every audit type. I would start with intake, where claims and related context enter through standard interfaces and are normalized into a common domain model. I would want the platform to understand the claim, lines, member, provider, contract, policy, eligibility, prior history and supporting documentation. Next is selection and prioritization. Different clients should be able to configure rules, analytics, ML scores, dollar thresholds and audit concepts without changing the underlying product. Then comes work orchestration. The platform should automatically decide whether a case can be processed straight through, needs a coder, needs a clinician, needs a fraud investigator, or requires more documentation. Optum already uses AI-supported claim selection and human expert review, which is the model I would build around. The reviewer workspace should then bring the evidence into one place rather than making people search across five systems. That could include the claim, medical record, contract terms, coding or clinical guidance, previous findings and relevant provider history. AI can summarize and highlight evidence, but the reviewer should see the source and remain accountable where judgment is required. Once a decision is made, the platform should capture not just 'approve' or 'deny,' but the reason, rule version, evidence, reviewer, timestamp and financial impact. Appeals then become another configurable state in the same case lifecycle. If an appeal overturns the original finding, that should flow directly back into rule and model monitoring. For post-pay cases, recovery should track outreach, offsets, repayments, aging and realized savings. Finally, reporting should work at three levels: operational metrics such as queue size and cycle time, effectiveness metrics such as audit yield and overturns, and financial metrics such as prevented, validated and recovered savings. The key design choice for me is configurability. Client-specific differences should live in rules, workflow configuration, roles, thresholds and content, not in different code branches. That is the same philosophy I have used in enterprise software. Reusable configuration and integration capabilities helped us migrate 13 workflows and significantly reduce repeated implementation effort. In a payment-integrity product, that same discipline is what lets you scale to more clients, more lines of business and new audit concepts without making the platform progressively harder to maintain.
**Recommendation:** build one workflow engine with a canonical domain model and versioned configuration, launch with two audit types, and judge success by how few code changes a third audit type needs.

### Q1.7.6 [Conceptual] How do pre-pay claim editing, clinical claim review, fraud/waste/abuse detection, contract compliance, third-party liability, and post-pay recovery differ? Where should each be used?
*Source: Top 70 document, question 11.*
I separate them based on the type of question we are trying to answer. Claim editing asks whether the claim follows coding, billing, reimbursement and policy rules. It is usually best suited to high-volume, deterministic or strongly evidence-based checks before payment. Optum positions its Claims Edit System specifically as an automated pre-pay capability. Clinical claim review is different because now the question may be whether the services documented in the medical record support what was billed. That often requires clinical context and human judgment, so I would use predictive analytics to select the right claims and then route them to certified coders or clinicians. Optum uses this combination of analytics, AI augmentation and human review across both pre- and post-pay settings. Fraud, waste and abuse detection is more behavioral. Instead of asking whether one line on one claim is incorrect, you may be looking for unusual provider patterns, relationships, identity risks or schemes across many claims. That is where network analytics, anomaly detection and investigations become important. Optum is increasingly moving this upstream as well, even validating providers at enrollment and continuously monitoring risk. Contract compliance asks whether the claim was priced and reimbursed according to the negotiated payer-provider agreement. That can be hard because contracts are complicated and frequently change, which is why digitizing contract terms into structured data is so useful. Third-party liability and coordination of benefits ask a completely different question: are we even the payer who should pay first? CMS defines COB around establishing which plan has primary payment responsibility when someone has multiple coverage. Optum supports that prospectively, pre-pay and post-pay. Finally, post-pay recovery is what I use when an incorrect payment has already happened. I still need it because there will always be errors that require hindsight or additional data, but ideally it becomes the last line of defense, not the dominant strategy. The important product principle is that these should not behave like six disconnected products. They are different decision capabilities acting on the same underlying claim lifecycle, so I would want common data, workflow, evidence, case management and feedback mechanisms underneath them.

### Q1.7.7 [Technical] How would you decide which claims should be automatically cleared, automatically edited, routed to a coder/clinician, or escalated for deeper investigation?
*Source: Top 70 document, question 12.*
**Clarifying questions:** What is the cost of an error by claim type? What are today's auto-adjudication and overturn rates? Which reviewers (coders, clinicians, SIU) are available and how many? Which actions are reversible?

**Assumptions:** four outcomes (clear, auto-edit, expert review, investigation); thresholds set by confidence, dollar value and reversibility; a clinician or coder always reviews medical-necessity and adverse decisions.

**Framework:** a decision hierarchy scored on confidence, risk, complexity and reversibility, with an autonomy ladder (suggest, draft, act with sampling, act broadly).

**Answer (spoken):**
I would think about this as a decisioning hierarchy built around confidence, risk, complexity and reversibility. The first category is claims we should simply clear. If all deterministic checks pass, the claim matches policy and contract logic, there are no significant risk signals, and the model confidence is high that the claim is clean, then I want straight-through processing. There is no value in creating friction where none is needed. The second group is claims where the issue is deterministic enough to act automatically. A duplicate, an impossible code combination or a clearly applicable contractual rule may be suitable for an automated edit, provided the rule is current, explainable and has a very low overturn rate. The third group is where the system sees a strong signal but judgment is required. That is where I would route to the right expert. A coding issue goes to a certified coder. A question involving medical necessity or clinical documentation goes to a clinician. Optum follows a similar pattern in claim review, using predictive scoring to identify claims with a high likelihood of error and then having coders and clinicians evaluate targeted cases. AI can make that review much faster by extracting relevant evidence from medical records, which Optum is already doing with LLM-based augmentation, but the expert remains responsible for the clinical decision. The fourth category is deeper investigation. That could involve unusual provider behavior, linked entities, possible fraud or a pattern that cannot be explained by one claim alone. Those cases should move into an investigative workflow with broader data and potentially SIU involvement. The decision thresholds should not be static. I would continuously monitor precision, false positives, appeal rates, overturns, reviewer acceptance and financial outcome by pathway. If automated edits start getting overturned, confidence thresholds go up or the rule returns to human review. If humans repeatedly approve a class of cases with very high consistency, that may become a candidate for additional automation. This is similar to how I approach AI products generally. You start with assistance, collect evidence on quality, then progressively automate where confidence and controls justify it. That gives you scale without treating every decision as equally safe to automate.
**Recommendation:** start in assist mode with measured accuracy by pathway, automate only where overturn rates are very low, and review thresholds monthly.

### Q1.7.8 [Conceptual] How would you reduce repeat billing/payment errors after an audit identifies a valid finding?
*Source: Top 70 document, question 13.*
The biggest missed opportunity in post-pay audit programs is treating the recovered dollar as the end of the workflow. I would treat a validated finding as new product intelligence. Once the audit confirms an error, I would first classify the root cause. Was it a provider coding mistake? A payer configuration issue? An unclear policy? A contract interpretation problem? Wrong eligibility or coordination of benefits? A system integration defect? Or potentially intentional behavior? Then I would look at recurrence. If this is one isolated mistake, recovery may be enough. If we see the same issue repeatedly from one provider, one specialty or across many payers, the product should move from recovery into prevention. Optum makes this same point in its claim-review strategy, which is designed not just to identify errors but also to reduce repeat errors across the payment lifecycle. For provider-side mistakes, I would build targeted education or pre-submission guidance so the claim is corrected before it enters adjudication. Optum's Coding Advisor and Real Edit Intelligence are examples of that approach. If it is a contract issue, I would update the digitized terms and apply the correct pricing logic pre-pay. If it is an internal adjudication defect, I would fix the configuration and identify historical claims that might also be affected. If it is COB, update eligibility information early enough that future claims route to the correct primary payer. Optum's TPL capability specifically sends confirmed coverage information back to clients to prevent future overpayments. From a product perspective, I would create a 'finding-to-prevention' workflow. Every confirmed finding should have an owner, root-cause category, recurrence score, preventive action and effectiveness measurement. Then I would track the percentage of savings concepts that migrate from post-pay to pre-pay or pre-submission over time. This is very similar to how I have approached recurring enterprise-software problems. In one of my platforms, we stopped treating implementation issues as customer-by-customer problems and converted them into reusable workflows and templates, which helped reduce implementation time by 75%. My instinct here would be the same: if the organization keeps solving the same error repeatedly, that is no longer just an operations problem. It is a product opportunity.

### Q1.7.9 [Conceptual] What are the major sources of complexity in U.S. claims payment, and how should a product manage that complexity?
*Source: Top 70 document, question 14.*
What makes claims payment difficult is that there is no single rulebook. Several rule systems interact on the same claim. First you have clinical and coding complexity. Diagnosis codes, procedure codes, modifiers and clinical documentation all have to line up. Optum notes that claim review operates in an environment with more than 85,000 diagnosis codes. Then you have reimbursement complexity. The same service can be reimbursed differently depending on whether you are dealing with fee-for-service, DRG, APC, capitation or another payment methodology. Next comes contract complexity. Two providers performing the same service can legitimately be paid differently because their agreements have different rates, carve-outs, thresholds or bundled-payment provisions. Optum points out that contracts can sit across PDFs, Word files and images and change frequently, which makes manual interpretation difficult. Then you have coverage and eligibility. Was the member covered on that date? Is the service included in the benefit? Was authorization required? You also have coordination of benefits, where another insurer may be primary. CMS specifically defines COB as determining which plan has primary payment responsibility when multiple plans cover the same person. And finally, all of this sits inside a regulatory environment that changes across Medicare, Medicaid, commercial business, states and federal requirements. For example, CMS is now requiring impacted payers to implement FHIR-based APIs for prior authorization and other data exchange, with key API requirements generally beginning in 2027. The product answer, in my view, is to separate stable platform capabilities from changing business content. I do not want regulatory rules, contracts or clinical policies hard-coded throughout the application. I want a common claim model, versioned rules and content, configurable workflows, effective dates, traceability and strong audit history. I would also separate deterministic rules from AI-based recommendations and preserve source provenance so a reviewer can always answer, 'Why did the system make this recommendation, and which version of the policy or contract did it use?' That architecture allows the platform to absorb change without continuously rebuilding itself. In complex enterprise software, that distinction between platform and configuration is one of the biggest determinants of whether a product scales.

### Q1.7.10 [Technical] How would you build the business case for moving a major payment-integrity workflow from post-pay recovery to pre-pay or pre-submission prevention?
*Source: Top 70 document, question 15.*
**Clarifying questions:** Which audit concept is the candidate? What share of its findings is deterministic? What are the current post-pay baseline, overturn rate and recovery rate? What integration is needed to run it pre-pay?

**Assumptions:** one high-volume, high-confidence concept; a pilot of one quarter; the full economic model is in the answer to the economics question later in this set (Q3.11.6).

**Framework:** baseline, options (post-pay, pre-pay, pre-submission), benefits (leakage avoided, operating cost, cash timing, provider friction), costs, risks, staged decision.

**Answer (spoken):**
I would build the case around net economic value, not simply 'we can catch this earlier.' I would start with the current post-pay baseline. How many claims are affected? What is the gross identified overpayment, validated amount and actual recovery? How long does recovery take? What does the audit cost? What percentage is appealed or overturned? How many provider contacts and medical-record requests are created? Once I understand the real cost of the existing process, I can estimate what changes if the same issue is caught before payment. The first obvious benefit is avoiding leakage. In post-pay, you identify an overpayment but you may not recover all of it, and there is a delay before cash comes back. Pre-pay prevents that cash from leaving in the first place. The second benefit is operational. You remove part of the investigation, outreach and recovery workload. The third is provider experience. If I can move even further left and correct the issue before claim submission, I may avoid the denial, resubmission and appeal entirely. Optum explicitly describes post-pay as creating rework, delayed savings, administrative overhead and provider abrasion, while real-time pre-submission solutions can reduce those costs. Then I would model the investment side: integration work, new rules or models, workflow change, provider connectivity, change management, false-positive risk and ongoing maintenance. I would probably run a controlled pilot on one high-volume, high-confidence audit concept before asking for a major platform investment. The success metrics would be prevented dollars, net savings after implementation and operating cost, reduction in post-pay cases, appeal and overturn rates, provider friction, processing time and accuracy. There is evidence that some upstream opportunities can be meaningful. For example, Optum says digitizing contract terms and using them during payment-integrity workflows can potentially improve medical-cost savings by up to 20%, although I would treat that as an Optum-specific modeled outcome rather than assume every use case delivers the same result. The way I would present the decision to leadership is simple: post-pay tells us what went wrong; prevention turns that learning into a recurring economic advantage. If the expected prevented savings, lower operating cost and lower provider friction exceed the implementation and change costs with an acceptable payback period, that is a much stronger investment than continuing to scale recovery operations.
**Recommendation:** approve a funded pilot on one concept with explicit gates (prevented dollars, false-positive rate, provider disputes), and scale only if the net economics beat continued post-pay recovery.
