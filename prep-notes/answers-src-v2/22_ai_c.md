## A2.7 Agentic AI design, security and business value (additional set)

### Q2.7.1 [Technical] Identify a high-value Agentic AI use case in payment integrity. What would the agent do, which tools/data could it access, and what decisions would remain human-controlled?
*Source: Top 70 document, question 36.*
**Clarifying questions:** Who are the reviewers and what do they do today? Which systems hold claims, policy, contracts, records and history? What decisions are regulated? What accuracy bar applies?

**Assumptions:** an internal claim-investigation agent assisting coders and clinicians; read-only tools at launch; a human approves every adverse or high-dollar decision.

**Framework:** AI use-case fit test (task, data, risk, value), then an autonomy ladder by decision type.

**Answer (spoken):**
One use case I find particularly compelling is an AI-assisted claim-investigation agent. Today, a reviewer may need to move between the claim, medical records, payment policies, provider contracts, coding guidance, authorization information, historical claims and earlier findings before they can even begin making a decision. That is exactly the kind of fragmented knowledge-work problem where an agent can create value. I would have the agent start from a selected claim, understand why it was flagged, retrieve the relevant policy and contract terms, identify supporting sections of the medical record, check prior claims or provider patterns, and then assemble a structured case summary with the potential issue, supporting evidence, confidence and recommended next step. It could call specific tools for contract lookup, policy retrieval, claim history, eligibility, authorization and evidence search, but each tool would have narrowly defined permissions. Optum is already applying parts of this model. It uses LLMs to turn provider contracts into structured data and AI to surface relevant medical-record evidence for human claim reviewers. Where I would be careful is the decision boundary. The agent can gather evidence, prioritize, explain and recommend, but I would retain human approval for clinical determinations, adverse decisions, high-value recoveries, suspected fraud referrals, new or low-confidence audit concepts and policy exceptions. That is consistent with how Optum approaches AI elsewhere. InterQual Auth Accelerator can automate supported approvals, but it explicitly does not automatically issue denials. I have built a similar pattern outside payment integrity. On a GenAI scientific assistant, we combined retrieval, embeddings, reranking and response validation across more than 1,000 technical documents and achieved over 95% retrieval precision, while SME validation and guardrails remained part of the product design. That experience shaped my view of agents: the goal is not maximum autonomy. It is to remove low-value searching and coordination so the expert spends more time on the judgment only the expert should make.
**Recommendation:** launch the agent in "assemble the case file" mode, measure reviewer time saved and agreement, and widen only the low-risk parts.

### Q2.7.2 [Technical] Design a RAG-based Policy Inquiry or Prior Authorization agent. How would you handle retrieval, grounding, source freshness, citations, permissions, and conflicting policies?
*Source: Top 70 document, question 37.*
**Clarifying questions:** Who asks (staff, providers, members)? Which plans, states and effective dates? Is the output an answer only, or an action? How often do policies change?

**Assumptions:** an internal policy and prior authorization inquiry agent; answers only; versioned policy corpus with effective dates.

**Framework:** layered RAG (ingest with metadata, filter by context, hybrid retrieve, rerank, generate with citations, validate, abstain), plus permission-aware retrieval.

**Answer (spoken):**
I would start with the knowledge layer because a policy agent is only as trustworthy as what it retrieves. I would ingest approved payer policies, benefit documents, authorization guidelines and relevant clinical criteria, but I would never treat them as an undifferentiated document library. Every chunk needs metadata such as payer, plan, line of business, state, procedure or service category, effective date, expiration date, policy version and source authority. At query time, the first step is understanding the context: which member, plan, date of service, CPT or procedure, diagnosis and geography are we talking about? Then I would use those attributes to filter the eligible source set before doing semantic retrieval. I would probably combine lexical and vector retrieval, then rerank the candidate evidence. The model should generate the answer only from the retrieved content and return citations to the exact policy passages that support it. If evidence is missing, I want the agent to say it cannot determine the answer rather than invent one. Freshness is critical because a perfectly grounded answer to an expired policy is still wrong. I would therefore version every source and make effective dates part of retrieval, with automated ingestion and validation when policies change. Permissions need to be applied before retrieval, not after generation, so the model never sees content the user is not entitled to access. Conflicting policies need a deterministic hierarchy where possible, for example member-specific or plan-specific policy over generic policy, current over expired, and regulatory requirements where applicable. If two valid authoritative sources genuinely conflict, the agent should surface both and escalate rather than deciding silently. Optum is already pursuing this kind of architecture. Its prior-authorization AI roles explicitly call for RAG using payer policies, clinical evidence and authorization guidelines, while Digital Auth Complete retrieves current medical policies and checks clinical documentation against them. I have worked on a comparable RAG problem where vector search, embeddings, reranking and validation produced over 95% document-retrieval precision. The lesson for me was that the model is only one component. Most of the trust comes from retrieval quality, metadata, provenance and knowing when not to answer.
**Recommendation:** ship with citations and an "I cannot determine this" path, measure groundedness and abstention accuracy, and treat content versioning as a product feature.

### Q2.7.3 [Technical] How would you evaluate an LLM or AI agent before production? Define offline and online metrics for accuracy, groundedness, hallucination, task completion, latency, cost, safety, and business outcomes.
*Source: Top 70 document, question 38.*
**Clarifying questions:** What decision does the AI influence? What is the cost of an error? What human baseline exists? Which data can be used for evaluation?

**Assumptions:** an agent assisting prior authorization or claim review in a regulated setting.

**Framework:** evaluation pyramid (retrieval, generation, agent behavior, safety, operations, online business outcomes) with launch, expansion and rollback thresholds.

**Answer (spoken):**
I would evaluate it in layers because no single 'accuracy' score tells me whether an AI product is production-ready. Before deployment, I would create a representative gold-standard evaluation set with domain experts covering normal cases, edge cases, conflicting information, missing evidence, ambiguous questions and deliberately unsafe inputs. At the retrieval layer I would measure whether the right evidence is being found, so precision, recall and ranking quality matter. At the generation layer I would measure factual correctness, groundedness, citation accuracy and unsupported-claim rate. For an agent, I would add task-completion rate, correct tool selection, correct sequencing, number of unnecessary tool calls and whether it terminates appropriately rather than looping. Then I want operational metrics such as p95 latency, token and inference cost per successful task, failure rate and scalability. Safety needs separate evaluation: PHI leakage, inappropriate data access, prompt injection resilience, policy violations and unsafe actions. Once those thresholds are met, I would deploy gradually to a controlled production cohort and start measuring online behavior. Human acceptance and override rate become very useful because domain experts are now telling us whether recommendations are actually usable. I would also track escalation rate, repeat attempts, abandonment and user trust. Finally, I would connect the AI metrics to the business problem. In prior authorization that could mean turnaround time, reviewer productivity and first-pass accuracy. In payment integrity it could mean audit yield, review cycle time, false positives, appeal rates and net savings. Optum's current Payer Agentic Platform role explicitly calls out agent accuracy, performance, scalability, operational efficiency and business impact as product KPIs. I've used a similar mindset on GenAI products. For a global pharma company, we did not stop at 'the model generates content.' Iterative UAT measured usefulness, with roughly 70% of generated content judged useful and 100% positive experience feedback in the documented testing, while guardrails and approved claims were part of the evaluation. For me, production readiness means the AI is good enough technically, operationally and economically, not just impressive in a demo.
**Recommendation:** gate launch on a domain-expert evaluation set, run shadow mode first, and report business outcomes alongside AI metrics.

### Q2.7.4 [Conceptual] Where would you place human-in-the-loop controls for AI that influences claim edits, audit findings, prior authorization, or recovery decisions?
*Source: Top 70 document, question 39.*
I would make the amount of human oversight proportional to the risk and reversibility of the action. I would not use one rule such as 'humans review every AI output,' because then we lose most of the value of automation. But I also would not say, 'the model is 97% accurate, so let it act everywhere.' For low-risk, deterministic situations where rules are well established and outcomes are easily reversible, I am comfortable with higher automation. A system might automatically clear a claim, route work, collect documentation, summarize evidence or recommend that an authorization requirement is satisfied. As we move toward actions that materially affect payment or patient care, I would increase human control. A novel audit finding, a low-confidence contract interpretation, a suspected fraud case, a high-dollar recovery or an adverse prior-authorization decision should go to an appropriately qualified reviewer. Optum's InterQual Auth Accelerator is a very good model of this principle. It can range from AI-guided review to auto-authorization, but Optum states that it never automatically issues a denial, keeping adverse patient-care decisions with humans. It also logs AI recommendations and interactions for compliance and refinement. In payment integrity, Optum similarly uses AI to select and augment claims while certified coders and clinicians remain responsible for targeted reviews. I would also make the human control visible in the workflow. The reviewer should see the recommendation, confidence, exact supporting evidence and what the AI did, rather than receiving an unexplained score. Their accept, modify or reject action should feed back into evaluation. I have taken that approach in my own AI products as well. On the scientific GenAI assistant, responsible-AI guardrails and SME validation were built into the product and helped achieve formal scientist approval alongside greater than 95% retrieval precision. The principle I would bring to Optum is simple: automate the work, not the accountability.

### Q2.7.5 [Conceptual] When would you use deterministic rules, traditional ML, an LLM, RAG, a single agent, or multi-agent orchestration?
*Source: Top 70 document, question 40.*
I would choose the simplest technology that solves the problem reliably. If the business logic is explicit and stable, I prefer a deterministic rule. Something like 'this code combination is not allowed under this policy' does not need an LLM. It should be explainable, testable and predictable. I would use traditional machine learning when the problem is probabilistic and the value is in recognizing patterns across structured historical data, for example predicting which claims are most likely to contain an error or identifying anomalous provider behavior. Optum already uses predictive scoring for claim selection. I would use an LLM when the problem involves language or unstructured information, such as interpreting provider contracts, summarizing medical records or explaining a complex finding. Optum's Contract Intelligence is a good example, turning unstructured provider agreements into structured, actionable data. RAG becomes valuable when the answer has to come from a controlled knowledge base that changes over time, such as payer policies or prior-authorization guidelines. That allows me to ground the LLM in current approved content instead of relying on what it learned during training. I would use a single agent when one goal requires several tool calls or steps but the workflow is still coherent, for example investigating a claim by retrieving policy, contract and history before drafting a recommendation. Multi-agent orchestration is something I would introduce carefully. It makes sense when there are genuinely distinct roles, perhaps a policy agent, clinical-evidence agent and contract agent that each have separate tools and reasoning boundaries, with another orchestrator coordinating the overall task. Optum's current AI hiring explicitly references both agentic workflows and multi-agent orchestration, so this is clearly an active direction. But I would not create five agents just because the architecture looks sophisticated. Every additional agent adds latency, cost, failure modes and debugging complexity. My rule is rules where certainty exists, ML where patterns matter, LLMs where language matters, RAG where trusted knowledge matters, and agents only where multi-step action genuinely creates value.

### Q2.7.6 [Technical] What reusable capabilities belong in an enterprise agentic platform: model gateway, RAG, runtime, orchestration, tool registry, identity, memory, evaluation, observability, governance, APIs, and lifecycle management?
*Source: Top 70 document, question 41.*
I would design the platform around what every AI-product team should not have to rebuild. At the bottom, I want a model gateway that abstracts approved models, credentials, routing, quotas and potentially model selection so applications are not tightly coupled to one provider. Then a shared retrieval layer should provide document ingestion, chunking, embeddings, vector search, reranking, metadata filtering and permission-aware RAG. Above that is the agent runtime and orchestration layer: create an agent, give it a defined goal, approved tools, execution limits, state and lifecycle, and let the platform manage invocation, retries, timeouts and scaling. I would have a governed tool registry because tool access is where agentic systems become genuinely powerful and genuinely risky. Each tool should have a clear schema, owner, permission model and audit trail. Identity should propagate from the user through the agent to downstream systems so an agent cannot silently operate with broader privileges than the person requesting the action. Memory should be treated carefully. Short-lived task state is useful, but long-term memory needs explicit purpose, retention and privacy rules. Then I would build evaluation into the platform rather than asking every product team to invent its own testing framework. Teams should be able to define golden datasets, quality metrics, regression tests, safety checks and model comparisons. Observability should show prompts, retrieval, tool calls, latency, token usage, cost, errors and final outcomes while protecting PHI. Governance should cover approved models, risk classification, policy controls, auditability and human-approval requirements. Optum's own Payer Agentic Platform role describes almost exactly this direction: reusable services for payer agents, grounding, retrieval, orchestration, interoperability, governance and repeatable evaluation. This also fits how I think about enterprise platforms generally. In my current work, moving common functionality into reusable integration and observability capabilities helped reduce implementation effort, cut diagnosis effort by around 40% and reduce downtime by 30%. The platform succeeds when a product team can go from a healthcare problem to a safe production agent substantially faster because identity, RAG, evaluation, security, monitoring and runtime are already solved well once.

### Q2.7.7 [Conceptual] How would you implement Responsible AI in a regulated healthcare product without slowing delivery to a halt?
*Source: Top 70 document, question 42.*
I would make Responsible AI part of the delivery path rather than a committee that appears two weeks before launch. The easiest way to slow AI development is to leave governance until the end and discover that nobody knows what evidence is required. I would start by classifying use cases by risk. A chatbot summarizing internal product documentation is not the same risk as an AI recommendation that can influence a clinical denial or financial recovery. The higher the risk, the stronger the requirements around validation, explainability, human approval and monitoring. Then I would create a paved road for teams: approved model providers, security patterns, RAG components, evaluation templates, standard privacy controls, model documentation, audit logging and predefined human-in-the-loop patterns. That actually speeds delivery because teams are not reinventing compliance for every use case. NIST's AI Risk Management Framework is helpful here because it treats AI risk as a continuous lifecycle organized around Govern, Map, Measure and Manage, rather than a one-time certification event. Before production, I would require evidence that the system meets use-case-specific quality and safety thresholds, that data use is appropriate, that known failure modes have been tested and that the fallback path is clear. After launch, monitoring and periodic re-evaluation continue because models, data and user behavior change. Optum itself is taking this approach. Its Payer Agentic Platform role explicitly includes Responsible AI, transparency and governance throughout the product lifecycle, while InterQual logs AI interactions and keeps humans responsible for adverse decisions. I have used the same philosophy on GenAI products. In the scientific assistant, multi-prompt validation, retrieval-quality measurement and SME approval were part of the product rather than an afterthought, while the pharma GenAI workflow incorporated approved claims, MLR reviewers and model-quality controls. Responsible AI, to me, is not the opposite of speed. Done well, it gives teams a repeatable way to move quickly without gambling with trust.

### Q2.7.8 [Technical] How would you defend an enterprise RAG/agent solution against prompt injection, PHI leakage, unauthorized tool use, cross-tenant data exposure, and unsafe actions?
*Source: Top 70 document, question 43.*
I would design security on the assumption that the model can be manipulated, because relying on the prompt to enforce security is not enough. First, user authentication and authorization must happen outside the LLM, and every downstream tool call should independently verify whether that user or agent identity is allowed to perform the requested action. OWASP specifically warns about excessive agency, where an agent has more functionality, permission or autonomy than it actually needs, and recommends enforcing authorization in downstream systems rather than trusting the model to decide. Second, I would apply least privilege to both data and tools. If a policy agent only needs read access, it should not receive write or delete capabilities. If an agent is working for Client A, retrieval filters and data-layer permissions must prevent Client B's information from ever entering its context. That separation belongs in the vector/data store and authorization layer, not merely in a system prompt. Third, I would minimize PHI before it reaches the model. HHS's minimum-necessary principle is a useful product design rule: use only the information required for the task. For external model endpoints, I would also verify retention, training use, encryption and contractual handling of healthcare data. Prompt injection needs multiple controls: distinguish trusted system instructions from untrusted retrieved content, scan inputs and retrieved documents, constrain tool calls, validate outputs and never allow retrieved text to grant itself new privileges. OWASP lists prompt injection and sensitive-information disclosure among the major LLM application risks. For high-impact actions, I would require an explicit approval step. An agent may draft a recovery action, but the user approves it. Finally, everything needs auditability: user, model, retrieved sources, tools invoked, parameters, outputs and resulting actions. I think of the security model as concentric boundaries. Even if one layer fails, the next one limits the blast radius. The model can recommend, but the system around the model decides what data it may see and what actions it may take.

### Q2.7.9 [Technical] How would you monitor an AI system in production for retrieval degradation, hallucinations, model drift, agent loops/failures, cost spikes, and poor user trust?
*Source: Top 70 document, question 44.*
I would monitor an AI product on four levels because infrastructure health alone tells me very little about whether the AI is behaving well. The first is system health: latency, error rate, throughput, model endpoint failures, vector-database availability and token or GPU consumption. The second is AI quality. For RAG I would track retrieval hit rate, relevance scores, citation coverage, empty retrievals and whether the correct authoritative source was found. For generation I would sample groundedness, unsupported claims, answer completeness and refusal quality. The third is agent behavior: tool-call success, number of steps per task, retry counts, loops, incorrect tool selection, abandoned executions and escalation rate. If an agent normally completes a task in four tool calls and suddenly needs eleven, that is an early warning even if it eventually succeeds. Then comes business and user behavior, which I think is the most important layer: recommendation acceptance, human overrides, corrections, repeat queries, abandonment, user satisfaction and the operational KPI we originally built the product to move. If a model is technically accurate but reviewers regularly override it, I have a trust or workflow problem. I would maintain a stable evaluation set and rerun it whenever the model, prompt, retrieval pipeline or major knowledge source changes. I would also monitor production examples continuously because offline tests will never cover every real-world pattern. Optum's Payer Agentic Platform role explicitly calls out agent accuracy and performance, platform scalability, utilization, operational efficiency and business impact as ongoing metrics. This is an area where my platform experience transfers directly. I led an AI-native observability initiative that reduced diagnosis and debugging effort by about 40%, and an AI root-cause capability brought some investigations down from days to minutes. The lesson I took from that is that good monitoring should not simply tell you that something failed. It should let you reconstruct what the system saw, what it decided, what it did and whether that decision actually helped the user.

### Q2.7.10 [Technical] How would you prove an AI product is creating business value rather than merely producing impressive demos?
*Source: Top 70 document, question 45.*
**Clarifying questions:** Which workflow does the AI change? What is the baseline time, cost and error rate? What volume? What does human oversight cost?

**Assumptions (illustrative):** a task takes 12 minutes today at $45 an hour loaded cost; with AI assistance it takes 7 minutes; AI inference costs $0.40 and added review overhead $0.60 per task; 500,000 tasks a year.

**Framework:** baseline, hypothesis, controlled comparison, net value per task.

**Worked estimate:** today 12/60 × $45 = $9.00 per task; with AI 7/60 × $45 = $5.25; labor saved $3.75; minus $1.00 AI and oversight cost leaves **$2.75 net per task**; × 500,000 = **$1.375M a year**. *Sanity check:* the case holds only if quality is unchanged, so overturn and error rates must be tracked.

**Answer (spoken):**
I would define the business case before building the AI, because otherwise teams tend to launch something impressive and then go hunting for a metric that makes it look successful. I would start with the current baseline. How long does the task take today? How many people are involved? What is the error rate? What does each transaction cost? What financial or customer outcome are we trying to change? Then I would define a small number of value hypotheses. If I am building an AI-assisted claim-review tool, maybe the hypothesis is that we can reduce evidence-search time by 40%, increase reviewer throughput by 25% and maintain or improve audit accuracy without increasing overturns. Those become the reasons for the product. During a pilot I would compare AI-assisted users against the existing workflow or use a phased rollout with a strong baseline. I would measure model quality, but I would never present '92% accuracy' as the ROI. I want to know whether cycle time fell, whether users accepted the recommendations, whether fewer manual touches were needed and whether the financial outcome improved. Then I subtract the real cost: model inference, vector storage, engineering, human oversight, support and any increase in downstream error handling. The result I care about is net value per successful task, not token count or number of AI conversations. I have seen this discipline work in my own AI projects. A healthcare data-engineering initiative using GenAI reduced estimated effort from 4,091 hours to 3,082, generating more than 1,000 hours of effort savings while improving development efficiency by 25-30% per sprint. In another GenAI content platform, the measured business case included roughly 75% faster content creation and 35-45% lower selected production costs, rather than simply model usage. That is how I would approach AI at Optum too. The AI has earned its place in the product when something important becomes faster, cheaper, safer, more accurate or more scalable, and we can prove the causal connection.
**Recommendation:** report net value per task and quality guardrails monthly, and stop or redesign any AI feature that cannot show positive net value within two quarters.
