## A3.10 Platform product strategy (additional set)

### Q3.10.1 [Technical] Assume you have just taken ownership of Optum's payment-integrity platform. What would your three-year product vision and strategy be?
*Source: Top 70 document, question 16.*
**Clarifying questions:** Who are the clients and what do they pay for? What is the current mix of pre-pay, post-pay and pre-submission savings? What is the architecture and team capacity? Which regulatory dates apply?

**Assumptions:** a mature rules-based payment integrity platform with duplicated workflows and client-specific logic, strong demand for AI, and an executive goal of moving savings upstream.

**Framework:** Rumelt's strategy kernel (diagnosis, guiding policy, coherent actions) across three horizons (unify, intelligently automate, move upstream).

**Answer (spoken):**
If I took ownership of the platform, I would start with a fairly simple three-year vision: move payment integrity from a collection of audit and recovery capabilities into one intelligence and workflow platform that helps get the claim right as early as possible. That direction is already consistent with where Optum is going. Optum talks about moving from post-pay recovery toward pre-pay prevention and ultimately payment precision, where intelligence reaches the provider workflow before the claim is submitted.
I would probably think about the three years in stages rather than trying to transform everything at once. In year one, I would focus on the foundation. I would understand where we currently have duplicated workflows, client-specific logic, disconnected data, manual handoffs and technical debt. Then I would standardize the things that should be common across payment integrity: claim intake, case management, work queues, rules, evidence, decision history, appeals, reporting, identity and integrations. My goal in that first year would be to make the platform easier to configure and easier to operate, while improving reliability and reducing the amount of custom engineering needed for every new client or audit type.
That is a pattern I have already seen work. In my current environment, we moved 13 enterprise integration workflows onto newer frameworks and built reusable integration accelerators rather than repeatedly solving similar implementations. On another platform, simplifying configuration reduced implementation timelines by 75%.
Year two is where I would aggressively move intelligence upstream. I would take the highest-volume, highest-confidence findings coming from post-pay audits and turn them into pre-pay rules, models or provider-side guidance. I would also expand AI where it genuinely improves reviewer productivity, evidence retrieval, prioritization and decision quality. Optum's Claim Review product already combines predictive selection, AI support and expert coders and clinicians, with review outcomes feeding back into the models.
By year three, I would want the platform to behave much more like a real-time payment-accuracy network. Payers should be able to configure policies without engineering changes, providers should receive actionable guidance earlier, and new payment-integrity use cases should launch on common platform capabilities rather than requiring another standalone product.
The business measures would evolve with the strategy. I would look for more savings moving from post-pay to pre-pay and pre-submission, lower cycle time, higher audit precision, fewer appeals, faster client onboarding, greater platform reuse and lower provider abrasion.
So the three-year journey for me is really unify, intelligently automate, then move upstream. The end state is not simply a better audit platform. It is a platform that steadily reduces the number of incorrect claims that need auditing in the first place.
**Recommendation:** commit to the three-stage vision, and in the first 90 days publish the capability and customer map, the target KPI tree and the first two upstream concepts.

### Q3.10.2 [Conceptual] What is the difference between a product and a platform? How do you decide whether a capability should become a reusable platform primitive?
*Source: Top 70 document, question 17.*
I think the simplest distinction is that a product primarily solves a user problem, while a platform makes it easier for multiple products, teams or use cases to solve problems repeatedly.
For example, a claim-review product may help an auditor review a claim and determine whether there is a valid finding. That is a product experience. But underneath it, things like identity, workflow orchestration, case management, evidence storage, rules execution, notifications, audit history and reporting could potentially be used by many different payment-integrity products. Those are platform candidates.
The important part is that I would not turn something into a platform capability just because two teams happen to use something similar. I normally look for a few signals. Does the problem repeat across several products or customers? Is the underlying behavior genuinely common, or does it only look similar at a superficial level? Will centralizing it reduce total engineering effort? Can the capability have a clear service contract and owner? And can teams use it without constantly requesting changes from the central platform team?
I have dealt with this distinction quite a bit in my current work. In supply-chain products, individual applications needed integrations with different systems. We could have kept building integrations inside every product. Instead, we created reusable integration capabilities and migrated 13 enterprise workflows onto common frameworks. That reduced repeated implementation work and made deployments more consistent.
The same thing happened with observability. Instead of every product inventing its own monitoring approach, we invested in centralized telemetry and monitoring capabilities. That helped reduce debugging effort by about 40% and production downtime by 30%.
There is also a danger on the other side. If everything becomes a platform service, you create an internal bureaucracy where product teams move slower because every change depends on another team. So my rule is that a platform capability should create leverage. The second, third and tenth consumer should get value faster than the first one did.
I would measure that leverage through things like reuse across products, time to integrate, implementation effort avoided, adoption, reliability and the amount of duplicate functionality eliminated.
So I would say: a product delivers the outcome directly. A platform creates the reusable building blocks that make many outcomes cheaper, faster and more consistent to deliver.

### Q3.10.3 [Technical] How would you design one configurable, reusable platform that supports multiple clients, lines of business, policies, workflows, and audit types without creating code forks?
*Source: Top 70 document, question 18.*
**Clarifying questions:** How many clients and audit types today? How much of the behavior is configuration versus code? What is the release process? What isolation do clients require?

**Assumptions:** 10 clients, four audit types, a shared core with client-level configuration, strong tenant isolation required.

**Framework:** R-C-A-D-T, using a layered model (core, template by line of business, client configuration, extension points).

**Answer (spoken):**
The first thing I would avoid is designing around individual customers. I would start by finding what is structurally common across all of those use cases.
Whether I am doing professional claim review, facility review, contract compliance or post-pay recovery, there are usually a few concepts that keep repeating: a claim comes in, something determines whether it should be reviewed, work gets assigned, evidence gets gathered, a decision is made, that decision is documented, there may be an appeal or recovery process, and the outcome gets reported. Those become the backbone of the platform.
So I would create a common domain model around objects such as claim, claim line, provider, member, policy, contract, audit, finding, evidence, decision, appeal and recovery. On top of that, I would have a configurable workflow engine rather than hard-coded workflows. Different clients could define states, queues, roles, approval steps, SLAs and escalation rules without changing product code.
The same principle applies to rules. Coding policy, thresholds, contract terms and line-of-business differences should sit in versioned configuration and rules services wherever possible. Optum's Claims Edit System already describes the ability for health plans to tune editing thresholds, clinical validation criteria and contract-specific terms.
Then I would separate integrations from the business workflow. A client may have a different claims engine, document repository or recovery system, but the platform should communicate through stable APIs and adapters rather than embedding that client logic throughout the application.
I would also design tenant-level controls from the beginning. Configuration, permissions, workflows, thresholds, data access and reporting should all be tenant-aware, with strong isolation and auditability.
This is very similar to problems I have dealt with in enterprise SaaS. One of the big improvements we made in my current environment was shifting from repeated customer-specific implementations toward reusable templates, configurable workflows and integration accelerators. That contributed to a 75% reduction in implementation time and significantly improved the customer onboarding experience.
The test I would use is fairly straightforward: can I onboard a new client or introduce a new audit type mostly through configuration rather than a new development project? If the answer remains no after several clients, I probably haven't built a platform yet. I have built a collection of custom applications sharing the same repository.
**Recommendation:** adopt the "new audit type through configuration" test as the platform health gate, and track time to onboard a client and share of requests solved by configuration.

### Q3.10.4 [Conceptual] How do you define product boundaries? What belongs in the core platform, what should be configuration, what should be an extension, and what should remain client-specific?
*Source: Top 70 document, question 19.*
I would make that decision based on two things: how common the capability is and how often it needs to vary.
Something belongs in the core platform when almost every customer or workflow needs it and consistency creates value. Identity and access management is a good example. Audit logging, workflow state management, basic case management, notifications, telemetry and API management are other examples. I would not want five different teams implementing those differently.
Configuration is where the capability is common but the business behavior varies. For payment integrity, that might include audit thresholds, workflow steps, approval levels, roles, routing criteria, SLA values, payer policies or reporting dimensions. The underlying engine stays the same, but each client can configure its operating model.
Extensions are useful when the need is legitimate and reusable enough that I want to support it, but I do not want it embedded in the core platform. An external clinical model, a specialized pricing service or a proprietary third-party fraud engine could connect through APIs or plug-in interfaces.
Then there is true client-specific functionality. I would keep that category deliberately small. Sometimes a large customer has a contractual or regulatory requirement that genuinely does not generalize. I would support it when the business case is strong, but I would isolate it so that it does not contaminate the common product.
The way I normally pressure-test these decisions is by asking, 'If this customer disappeared tomorrow, would I still want this capability in the product?' If the answer is yes, it may belong in core or configuration. If the answer is no, I need a very strong commercial reason before putting it into the main platform.
I have seen the value of this distinction in my current role. We built industry-specific planning templates because enough customers shared the same structural problem. By turning those implementation patterns into configurable product capabilities rather than custom projects, we reduced onboarding timelines by 75% and improved customer satisfaction by 40%.
So I am very comfortable saying yes to an important customer need, but I always ask where that need should live architecturally. That's what protects the platform five years later.

### Q3.10.5 [Technical] How would you prioritize a roadmap when Business wants revenue features, Operations wants workflow improvements, Engineering wants modernization, Compliance wants controls, and customers want custom requirements?
*Source: Top 70 document, question 20.*
**Clarifying questions:** Which items are mandatory (regulatory, security)? What are the quarterly outcomes leadership has agreed? What capacity do we have? Which requests have contractual commitments?

**Assumptions:** one quarter, eight candidate initiatives, mandatory items already carved out, the rest scored on a shared model.

**Framework:** constraints first, then WSJF or weighted scoring across value, customer impact, efficiency, risk, reuse, confidence and effort, then a portfolio check.

**Answer (spoken):**
I have rarely worked on a roadmap where everyone wanted the same thing, so I actually expect this situation.
I would start by removing the idea that every stakeholder gets a percentage of the roadmap. Instead, I would get everyone aligned on the outcomes the product has to deliver. For a payment-integrity platform, those might include medical-cost savings, audit effectiveness, cycle time, client adoption, platform reliability, regulatory compliance and reduced provider friction. Optum's own Lead TPM role explicitly calls out measures such as throughput, cycle-time reduction, audit yield and adoption, so I would use that kind of outcome structure as the common language.
Then I would separate non-negotiable work from discretionary investment. A regulatory deadline, critical security vulnerability or severe reliability risk does not compete against a nice-to-have customer feature using the same scoring model. Those items become constraints.
For everything else, I would use a portfolio-level scoring discussion. I would look at expected customer or financial impact, number of customers affected, strategic alignment, reuse across the platform, urgency, confidence in the evidence and delivery cost. I would also explicitly include the cost of not doing something. Technical debt is a good example. It may not produce immediate revenue, but if it is causing outages, slowing every release or blocking future capabilities, its economic impact can be very large.
I have used essentially this approach in my current role, where I have had to balance enterprise customer requests, modernization and long-term technology investments for a product with more than $5M in ARR. At the broader portfolio level, those investment decisions contributed to more than $20M in ARR through product innovation.
I would also make the trade-offs visible. If leadership chooses a custom customer requirement over an architecture investment, I want everyone to see the consequence, not discover six months later that modernization slipped.
One of the things I learned through Voice of Customer programs is that the loudest request is not always the largest problem. Structured customer discovery helped us identify adoption barriers that ultimately contributed to a 40% improvement in customer satisfaction.
So my job isn't to make every stakeholder happy with every roadmap decision. My job is to make sure they understand why the decision maximizes the product's overall value and what trade-off we are consciously accepting.
**Recommendation:** publish the scored list with the assumptions and trade-offs, get one sponsor decision, and record what was deferred and why.

### Q3.10.6 [Conceptual] How do you balance platform stability, new capabilities, incremental workflow improvements, and technical debt across a multi-quarter roadmap?
*Source: Top 70 document, question 21.*
I don't believe in saying that every team should permanently spend 20% on technical debt or 30% on platform work. The allocation should change based on the health and maturity of the product.
I would start with evidence. What is our uptime? How many incidents are we seeing? How much engineering capacity disappears into support? Are releases becoming slower? Are customers complaining about performance? Are architectural limitations blocking new business? That tells me whether technical debt is just inconvenient or whether it has become a business problem.
I've had situations where modernization was clearly the right product decision. One of our enterprise platforms had scalability and reliability constraints. Instead of continuing to layer features on top, we modernized APIs and developer infrastructure toward cloud-native services. The outcome was a 30% reduction in production downtime, growth from roughly 40 concurrent users to more than 1,000, and approximately $900K in annual infrastructure savings. That is technical debt work, but the result is absolutely a product outcome.
At the same time, I would not stop feature development for a year while we rewrite everything. I prefer incremental modernization where possible. For example, if we are introducing a new claims-review workflow, I might use that investment to migrate the workflow onto the new orchestration layer rather than rebuilding every legacy workflow upfront.
So across a multi-quarter roadmap, I would have four visible investment categories: customer/business capabilities, workflow improvements, platform/reliability work and mandatory security/compliance. But the weighting would be dynamic.
I would also tie platform investment to specific outcomes. Instead of saying 'modernize the rules engine,' I would say 'reduce the time required to launch a new audit concept from eight weeks to two.' Instead of 'improve architecture,' I might target a 50% reduction in integration effort or a measurable availability improvement.
That makes the trade-off much easier for business stakeholders to understand.
For me, the real question is not features versus technical debt. It is short-term value versus the product's ability to continue delivering value at speed. A senior Product Manager has to protect both.

### Q3.10.7 [Conceptual] When would you build a capability internally, buy a vendor solution, partner with another Optum platform, or expose/consume an API?
*Source: Top 70 document, question 22.*
I would start with one question: does owning this capability create meaningful strategic differentiation for us?
If the capability directly drives why customers choose the product, contains proprietary domain intelligence or creates an important competitive advantage, I lean toward building it. In payment integrity, the decisioning logic, workflow intelligence or ways of combining claim data with clinical and contract knowledge may be strategically important enough to own.
If it is important but fairly commoditized, I would seriously consider buying it. Identity management is a good example. Observability infrastructure, certain document-processing components or commodity developer tooling may fall into the same category. Owning every layer of the technology stack usually creates more maintenance than differentiation.
Inside a company as large as Optum, there is a third choice that I think is especially important: reuse another internal platform. Before building something like an enterprise AI runtime, developer gateway or common eligibility service, I would ask whether another Optum team already provides it. The Principal TPM role for Optum's AI platform is explicitly focused on turning GPU compute, model serving, agent runtimes, orchestration and APIs into reusable enterprise services. That is exactly the kind of capability I would consume rather than recreate inside a payment-integrity product.
And then there are APIs. If I need a capability but don't need to own the implementation, an API is often the cleanest boundary. Optum itself is clearly moving in that direction. The Marketplace offers APIs, developer documentation and sandboxes so consumers can test capabilities before production.
My evaluation would include differentiation, time-to-market, TCO, security, data sensitivity, reliability, integration effort, switching cost, vendor lock-in and long-term ownership.
I've used the same thinking with enterprise integrations. Instead of embedding every external-system requirement into individual applications, we built reusable integration frameworks and migrated 13 workflows onto them.
So I wouldn't default to build or buy. I would choose the option that lets the product team spend its engineering energy on the capabilities customers will actually value us for.

### Q3.10.8 [Conceptual] How would you identify reusable capabilities across intake, workflow, rules, evidence, decisioning, reporting, identity, integrations, and configuration?
*Source: Top 70 document, question 23.*
I would start by looking at actual workflows, not by sitting in a room and inventing a platform architecture.
I would take several representative use cases, maybe professional claim review, facility review, contract compliance, data mining and recovery, and map each one end to end. Then I would look for the verbs and objects that repeat.
For example, every workflow may need to ingest something, validate it, assign it, gather evidence, apply rules, make a decision, document the outcome, communicate something and report the result. Those repeated behaviors are the first candidates for reusable capabilities.
Then I would distinguish common behavior from common-looking behavior. Two workflows may both have 'review,' but one may require a certified coder while another needs a clinician. The common primitive could therefore be task routing and evidence presentation, while the actual review criteria remain domain-specific.
I would then validate the abstraction with Engineering, Operations and several customers. If I cannot describe a capability with a stable contract that works for multiple use cases without dozens of exceptions, it probably isn't mature enough to become a platform primitive.
There is also an economic test. I want to know whether centralizing it genuinely saves us time. Does a new client get onboarded faster? Do engineering teams write less duplicate code? Does reliability improve? Can we launch another audit type faster?
I've followed that pattern in my current environment. We saw the same integration problems appearing across products, so rather than keep solving them independently, we developed reusable accelerators and moved 13 workflows onto next-generation integration frameworks. We did something similar with monitoring. By centralizing observability rather than having separate product-level approaches, we reduced issue diagnosis effort by about 40%.
I would apply exactly that thinking to payment integrity. Intake, workflow, evidence, identity and audit history are likely strong platform candidates. The clinical or coding intelligence that determines whether a particular claim is correct may remain specialized.
The goal isn't maximum reuse. The goal is reuse at the right abstraction level. If you abstract too little, you duplicate everything. If you abstract too aggressively, you build a generic platform that nobody enjoys using.

### Q3.10.9 [Technical] You inherit several overlapping legacy products serving similar customers. How would you rationalize them into a coherent platform without disrupting current clients?
*Source: Top 70 document, question 24.*
**Clarifying questions:** How many products, clients and integrations overlap? What contracts and SLAs are attached? What do the products cost to run and support? What is the end-state architecture?

**Assumptions:** three overlapping legacy products, about 40 clients, a goal of one platform in three years with no client disruption.

**Framework:** capability and customer map, target architecture, strangler migration in cohorts, decommission criteria.

**Answer (spoken):**
My first step would actually be to resist announcing a consolidation program too early. Before deciding which system survives, I need to understand why several products exist in the first place. Sometimes what looks like duplication is carrying ten years of client commitments, regulatory differences or workflows that are not obvious from the UI.
I would start with a capability and customer map. Which clients use each product? What capabilities do they actually use? Which integrations exist? What contracts and SLAs are attached? Which products have the strongest reliability and economics? And which ones are accumulating the highest support and engineering cost?
From there I would define the target architecture based on capabilities, not on whichever legacy product happens to have the biggest installed base.
I would almost certainly avoid a big-bang migration. I prefer what is often called an incremental or strangler approach. Build common services such as identity, workflow, rules or reporting, and gradually have legacy products consume those services. That lets the organization modernize underneath the customer experience before forcing customers through a major migration.
Then I would migrate in cohorts. I might start with new customers on the target platform, then customers with simple configurations, then more complex accounts. For strategic clients, I might run old and new workflows in parallel for a period and reconcile outputs before cutover.
Optum's own payment-integrity positioning is useful here because it explicitly talks about one coordinated but modular system rather than simply adding more point solutions.
I have been through a smaller version of this problem in my own platform work. We migrated 13 enterprise integration workflows from older processing frameworks into newer cloud-oriented integration patterns while continuing to support active customer environments. We also modernized APIs and infrastructure without stopping the product roadmap, ultimately reducing downtime by 30% and generating about $900K in annual infrastructure savings.
I would track migration through customer impact, feature coverage, transaction accuracy, incident rates, adoption and the percentage of traffic or clients running on the target architecture.
The end goal is not to say, 'We retired three systems.' The goal is that customers experience better reliability, faster innovation and less complexity, while the company removes duplicated technology behind the scenes.
**Recommendation:** move new clients to the target platform first, migrate simple accounts next, run parallel for strategic accounts, and retire a legacy product only when its traffic and clients reach zero.

### Q3.10.10 [Conceptual] How would you drive adoption of an internal or external platform when the underlying technology is strong but teams keep building their own solutions?
*Source: Top 70 document, question 25.*
If teams keep building their own solutions, I would assume the platform has a product problem before I assume the teams have a discipline problem.
The first thing I would do is talk to the teams choosing not to use it. Usually there is a reason. Maybe onboarding takes six weeks. Maybe the API documentation isn't clear. Maybe they can't get support. Maybe the platform solves 80% of what they need but the missing 20% is critical. Or perhaps teams don't understand what it can actually do.
I have seen this in enterprise products. In my current work, I conducted more than 50 customer discovery sessions specifically to understand adoption barriers around our platform experience. Those conversations helped us identify issues that would never have been obvious from a backlog alone, and the resulting improvements increased planner productivity by around 30%.
Once I understand the barriers, I would treat the platform like a product with an onboarding funnel. How long does it take a developer or customer to understand it? To get credentials? To run a first request? To integrate? To reach production? Where do they abandon?
I would then invest heavily in the developer and consumer experience. Clear APIs, strong documentation, sample implementations, SDKs where useful, self-service access, sandboxes, reference architectures, good error messages and predictable SLOs matter enormously. Optum Marketplace is already demonstrating this model with developer documentation, Swagger files, sample code and sandbox access that lets organizations test APIs before committing to production.
I would also create 'golden paths' for the most common use cases. Don't ask every team to assemble ten platform services themselves. Give them a reference journey that gets them from zero to a working solution quickly.
Then I would make the economics visible. If a team can launch in four weeks using the common platform versus four months building independently, that becomes a compelling adoption story.
Finally, I would measure time-to-first-value, onboarding completion, active consumers, API usage, reuse across products, support tickets and the percentage of teams choosing the platform without being mandated to.
That's the adoption metric I care most about. If people use the platform only because governance forces them to, the platform still has work to do. If they choose it because it is genuinely the fastest and safest way to build, then we have created a successful platform product.

## A3.11 Product metrics and business value (additional set)

### Q3.11.1 [Technical] What would be your North Star and KPI tree for a payment-integrity platform?
*Source: Top 70 document, question 46.*
**Clarifying questions:** Who is the economic buyer? Which savings do clients value (cost, accuracy, provider friction)? Pre-pay or post-pay? How is value verified?

**Assumptions:** a platform covering pre-pay edits and post-pay audits; realized net value is what clients pay for.

**Framework:** North Star plus input metrics and guardrails.

**Answer (spoken):**
I would avoid choosing gross savings as the North Star because it can create the wrong behavior. A team can increase identified savings simply by reviewing more claims or becoming more aggressive, while false positives, appeals and provider friction quietly get worse. My North Star would be something closer to net validated medical spend prevented or recovered per million claims, adjusted for operating cost and provider friction. The exact formula would depend on how Optum measures realized value today, but the principle is that we reward accurate economic value, not activity. Then I would build a KPI tree underneath it. The first branch is effectiveness: audit yield, true-positive rate, validated savings, realized savings and savings per claim reviewed. The second is operational efficiency: throughput, cycle time, touch time, queue aging and straight-through processing. The third is provider experience: appeals, overturns, record requests, repeat disputes and other abrasion indicators. The fourth is platform adoption and leverage: active users, active clients, reusable capability adoption, time to onboard a client and time to introduce a new audit concept. Finally I would track reliability and cost because none of the business metrics matter if the platform is unstable. What I like is that this is also very close to how Optum is defining success for the role. The Lead TPM job explicitly calls out throughput, cycle-time reduction, audit yield, user adoption, operational efficiency and financial impact. In my own platform work, I have learned to combine technical and business measures in the same way. For example, one modernization program was successful not because we completed an architecture migration, but because downtime fell 30%, scalability grew from roughly 40 concurrent users to more than 1,000, and annual infrastructure cost fell by about $900K. So for payment integrity, I would want the team to always be able to answer three questions together: Are we finding the right opportunities? Are we processing them efficiently? And are we creating real net value without pushing cost and frustration somewhere else in the system?
**Recommendation:** adopt net validated value per million claims as the North Star, with effectiveness, efficiency, provider experience and adoption as branches, and reliability and cost as guardrails.

### Q3.11.2 [Conceptual] Which metrics would you use to measure claim-review effectiveness, and why?
*Source: Top 70 document, question 47.*
I would measure claim review as a funnel because looking at one metric in isolation can be misleading. At the top of the funnel, I want to know whether we are selecting the right claims. That is where precision or true-positive rate matters. If we send ten claims to reviewers and only one produces a valid finding, we are creating cost and provider friction before the reviewer even starts. Optum itself uses predictive scoring to identify claims with a high likelihood of billing error before routing them to certified coders or clinicians, so selection quality is fundamental. The next metric is audit yield, which tells me what proportion of reviews generate valid findings, and then savings per reviewed claim tells me whether we are applying scarce reviewer capacity to economically meaningful opportunities. After that I look at efficiency: cycle time, reviewer touch time, claims per reviewer, medical-record retrieval time and queue aging. Then I want downstream quality metrics, especially appeals and overturns. A finding is not particularly valuable if it is routinely reversed after the provider challenges it. I would distinguish identified savings, validated savings and actually realized savings so we do not confuse opportunity with economic benefit. Finally, provider experience has to sit in the same dashboard. Medical-record request rates, appeal volumes and recurring disputes tell me whether we are making the process unnecessarily difficult. Optum explicitly positions Claim Review around maximizing savings while reducing repeat errors and minimizing provider abrasion. I also like targeted metrics by audit type because averages can hide a lot. Optum's Focused Claims Review, for example, reports that 55% to 65% of targeted claims reviewed show errors and that clients saved 17% to 22% of total claim dollars reviewed. I would use numbers like that as benchmarks for particular use cases, not as universal targets. The product question is always: are we selecting better, reviewing faster, making more defensible decisions and generating more realized value for every unit of effort?

### Q3.11.3 [Technical] Audit yield improves but appeals and overturns also rise. Is the product getting better or worse?
*Source: Top 70 document, question 48.*
**Clarifying questions:** Which concepts drove the yield increase? What is the overturn rate by concept? Did thresholds, models or guidance change? What are the review and dispute costs?

**Assumptions:** yield up, appeals and overturns also up, review cost unchanged.

**Framework:** cohort comparison and net-value calculation (upheld savings minus review and dispute cost).

**Answer (spoken):**
My answer would be that I do not know yet, and that is exactly why I would not optimize audit yield by itself. At first glance the team may celebrate because more reviews are producing findings. But if appeals and overturns are rising at the same time, we may simply have become more aggressive rather than more accurate. I would start by looking at the full cohort. Which audit concepts drove the increase in yield? Which ones drove the appeals? Are the same concepts also producing high overturn rates? Did we recently change thresholds, policy interpretation, model scores or reviewer guidance? Then I would follow the dollars through the lifecycle. Suppose we identify $10M more in savings, but a large portion is overturned, the provider-relations workload doubles and reviewer time rises significantly. That may be worse economically even though audit yield improved. I would calculate something closer to upheld or realized savings net of review and dispute costs, and compare that against the prior period. I would also segment by provider and claim type. Sometimes a new review concept is genuinely strong but documentation or communication is poor, so providers appeal because they do not understand the rationale. In that situation the issue may be transparency rather than decision quality. Optum makes this point in its claim-editing products by emphasizing clear edit rationale and sourcing, because transparent decisions can reduce unnecessary denials and provider abrasion. On the other hand, if the overturned findings cluster around one rule or model, I would pause or tighten that concept until we understand the failure. I have seen similar situations in enterprise products where a top-line metric improved but secondary indicators exposed a poorer user experience. My instinct is always to trace the complete outcome instead of rewarding the first successful event in the funnel. So I would tell leadership: audit yield tells us how often we find something; overturn rate tells us how often we were right after challenge. We need both before deciding whether the product has improved.
**Recommendation:** judge the product on upheld, net savings and dispute rate, tighten the concepts driving overturns, and improve the explanations given to providers before expanding.

### Q3.11.4 [Technical] A new workflow has high user adoption but little measurable financial or operational impact. How would you diagnose the gap?
*Source: Top 70 document, question 49.*
**Clarifying questions:** What outcome was this workflow supposed to move? Where is the bottleneck in the full journey? Is the metric horizon long enough? How is adoption defined (logins or decisions)?

**Assumptions:** high usage, flat cycle time and flat savings.

**Framework:** value hypothesis, step-by-step journey map, issue tree (wrong bottleneck, shallow usage, wrong metric, time lag).

**Answer (spoken):**
I would actually see that as useful information. High adoption tells me we probably solved a real usability or workflow problem, but it does not prove that we solved an important business problem. I would go back to the original value hypothesis. What did we expect users to do differently because this workflow existed? If the goal was to reduce audit cycle time, then did time actually move? If the goal was to increase savings, did we improve selection or throughput enough to change the financial outcome? Then I would look at the workflow step by step. Users may be using the new feature, but the real bottleneck could sit somewhere else. For example, we may have made audit assignment much faster while reviewers still wait two days for medical records, so total cycle time barely changes. Or adoption may be high because the tool is easier, but it replaces an already inexpensive manual step. I would also test whether the metric horizon is right. Some enterprise products create operational benefits quickly but financial impact only becomes visible after several claim cycles or recovery periods. Then I would look at behavior quality rather than just logins. Are users completing the intended workflow? Are they using the decision-support capability or simply entering the screen and continuing with the old process? Optum's role description makes this distinction important because it separates user adoption from operational efficiency, audit effectiveness and financial impact. I have dealt with this in other products. A feature can be popular without materially changing the outcome, which is why I prefer instrumenting the complete journey before launch. In one healthcare data-engineering initiative, for example, we did not measure success through Copilot logins. ### Q3.11.5 [Technical] How would you build a business case for a $10M platform modernization or AI investment?

### Q3.11.6 [Technical] How would you quantify the economics of moving an audit from post-pay recovery to pre-pay prevention?
*Source: Top 70 document, question 51.*
**Clarifying questions:** Which concept and how many claims? What are the validated, recovered and overturned rates? What is the cost of audit and recovery? What would pre-pay cost to run and integrate?

**Assumptions (illustrative):** post-pay identifies $20M a year; 80 percent validated ($16M); 87.5 percent of that recovered ($14M); audit and recovery cost $3M a year; recovery takes about 9 months on average; cost of capital 8 percent. Pre-pay would prevent $15M at an operating cost of $1.5M and a one-time $4M integration.

**Framework:** claim-for-claim comparison of net value, cash timing and provider friction.

**Worked estimate:**
- Post-pay net: $14M − $3M = **$11M** a year.
- Pre-pay net: $15M − $1.5M = **$13.5M** a year, so +$2.5M a year.
- Cash timing: $14M × 8 percent × 0.75 years = about $0.84M a year of value from not waiting for recovery.
- Total advantage: 2.5 + 0.84 = about **$3.3M a year**; payback on $4M = 4 / 3.34 = about **1.2 years**.
- *Sanity check:* the gain is driven mainly by lower operating cost and avoided leakage; if false positives double the pre-pay operating cost to $3M, the advantage falls to about $1.8M a year, so false-positive control is the key risk.

**Answer (spoken):**
I would compare the two models claim for claim, because the value is much broader than the headline savings. On the post-pay side, I would establish the gross overpayment identified, the percentage that is actually validated, the percentage successfully recovered, the average time to recovery and the cost of audit, provider outreach, appeals and collections. There is also a cash-timing element because the money has already left the payer. Then I would model the same opportunity in pre-pay. What percentage of those errors could realistically have been identified before adjudication? What would the false-positive rate be? What does it cost to integrate the check into the workflow? How much manual review would still be required? The biggest economic advantage is that prevented dollars do not need to be recovered later. You also remove some recovery operations, provider correspondence and rework. Optum describes exactly these disadvantages of post-pay: rework, delayed savings, administrative overhead and provider abrasion, which is why the company is moving intervention earlier. I would also measure how quickly the investment pays back. Suppose a post-pay concept identifies $20M annually, but historically only $16M is validated and $14M is ultimately recovered after operational costs. If a pre-pay version could reliably prevent $15M with lower operating cost and acceptable false positives, it may create greater net value even though the gross 'savings' headline initially looks smaller. Then I would consider going one step further upstream. If a recurring error can be corrected before submission, we may avoid both the incorrect payment and the denial or appeal. Optum's Cost Avoidance products are explicitly designed around correcting coding and billing issues before the claim is submitted. So the business case I would show is net prevented value + operating savings + cash-flow benefit + lower provider friction, minus technology and change cost, compared directly with the net economics of the existing recovery model.
**Recommendation:** pilot the concept pre-pay with a false-positive guardrail, then scale if the pilot confirms net advantage above the investment hurdle.

### Q3.11.7 [Conceptual] How would you establish leading indicators and lagging indicators for a complex enterprise healthcare product?
