## A5.1 People leadership

### Q5.1.1 [Experience] How have you managed and developed Product Managers? Give me an example of somebody whose performance materially improved because of your coaching.
*Source: Top 70 document, question 66 and 122. Questions 66 and 122 are the same question; the answer that avoids inventing a personal metric is used.*
My approach to developing PMs is to improve their judgment, not make them dependent on mine. I've led and mentored Product Managers, Business Analysts and other product practitioners across both o9 and Publicis Sapient, and one pattern I've seen is that people often understand the mechanics of Product Management before they become confident making difficult decisions. So when I coach someone, I focus on the quality of the questions they ask, how they use evidence, how clearly they communicate trade-offs and whether they can own a problem without escalating every decision. In one team, I had PMs who were strong at execution but initially relied heavily on me for prioritization and stakeholder conversations. I introduced clearer outcome ownership, more structured PRDs, common success metrics and regular product reviews where the PM had to present the problem, options and their recommendation. I also deliberately stopped stepping into some customer and Engineering conversations so they could build confidence, then we used one-on-ones to review what worked and what they would do differently. Over time, I saw the quality of their independent decisions improve and I could shift my own attention from day-to-day execution toward portfolio strategy. At the broader team level, the changes in ownership and product discipline contributed to around 25% better delivery efficiency, with related improvements in engineering productivity and defect resolution. I would be careful in an interview not to invent a personal percentage for one PM because I did not formally measure an individual that way. The evidence I have is the increased scope they could own and the measurable improvement in the team operating around them. That is also directly relevant to Optum, because this Lead role explicitly includes coaching PMs, setting expectations and supporting their career development.

### Q5.1.2 [Behavioral] How do you handle an underperforming Product Manager?
*Source: Top 70 document, question 69.*
I would start by resisting the label 'underperformer' until I understand why the person is struggling, because the intervention is very different depending on the cause. I normally look at five things: clarity, skill, judgment, capacity and motivation. Sometimes the PM genuinely doesn't understand what success looks like. Sometimes they are strong on execution but weak on discovery or stakeholder management. Sometimes the scope is simply too broad. And occasionally it is an accountability issue. I would start with specific evidence rather than general feedback. For example, instead of saying, 'your stakeholder management needs improvement,' I might say, 'in the last three roadmap reviews, decisions remained unresolved because the options and recommendation weren't prepared in advance.' Then I would agree on two or three observable expectations for the next six to eight weeks. I would increase coaching frequency temporarily, perhaps weekly one-on-ones, reviewing an upcoming customer conversation or roadmap decision before it happens and then discussing what worked afterward. I would also give them ownership rather than hovering over them, because people cannot demonstrate improvement if I keep taking the work back. I've used that broader coaching model across PM and engineering teams, combining clearer backlog ownership, stronger requirements discipline and more structured delivery practices. At team level, those changes contributed to about 25% higher delivery efficiency, 40% better engineering productivity and 25% faster defect resolution. If the person improves, I progressively reduce the coaching intensity and expand their scope. If the expectations remain clear, support has been provided and performance still doesn't move, then I would be equally comfortable having a direct conversation about fit and next steps. I think a manager owes people both things: a genuine opportunity to improve and complete clarity about the standard they are expected to meet.

### Q5.1.3 [Behavioral] How do you delegate across a PM team while retaining accountability for product outcomes and keeping strategy coherent?
*Source: Top 70 document, question 71.*
I think delegation works best when I delegate outcomes and decision areas, not just tasks. If I tell one PM to own the backlog and another PM to own customer meetings, I still remain the real Product Manager because they are just executing pieces of my work. Instead, I prefer giving a PM a coherent problem space with a clear outcome, boundaries and success metrics. For example, one PM may own Search and Discovery while another owns Cart and Checkout, but both understand the larger product strategy, customer segments and shared platform principles. Then I create a small number of operating mechanisms that keep the pieces coherent. We might have a weekly product leadership review for major decisions and dependencies, a monthly roadmap review against outcomes, shared product principles, and common metrics. I don't need to approve every story, but I do want visibility into decisions that affect another product area, introduce architectural commitments or materially change customer outcomes. In one of my leadership roles, I was coordinating multiple PMs alongside a broader Engineering and QA organization, and that experience taught me that over-centralizing decisions quickly makes the Lead PM the bottleneck. More broadly, when we introduced clearer ownership, reusable product practices and better cross-functional planning, delivery efficiency improved by roughly 25%. I still remain accountable for the overall portfolio outcome. If one area misses, I don't tell leadership, 'that belonged to another PM.' My responsibility is to create the system in which PMs can operate independently while risks surface early enough for me to help. The balance I aim for is simple: high autonomy inside clear strategic boundaries, and high transparency around decisions and outcomes.

### Q5.1.4 [Conceptual] How would you structure goals, operating cadence, career development, and performance expectations for a team of Product Managers?
*Source: Top 70 document, question 72.*
I would keep the operating model fairly simple because teams can spend so much time managing the product process that they have less time managing the product. At the beginning of a quarter, each PM should have a small number of outcome-oriented goals tied to the product strategy. I would expect them to know the customer problem they own, the business or operational metric they are trying to move and the major risks to that outcome. Then I would establish a predictable cadence. Weekly one-on-ones are for the person, not status reporting. We would talk about difficult decisions, stakeholder relationships, where they need coaching and where they want more scope. A separate weekly or fortnightly product review can cover roadmap decisions and cross-product dependencies. Monthly, I would review product outcomes rather than feature completion. Quarterly, I would step back and look at strategy, customer evidence and development goals. Career development would be individualized. One PM may need stronger technical depth, another executive communication, another customer discovery or people leadership. I would agree on one or two development themes and then deliberately create assignments where they can practice them. I have used this philosophy through mentoring PMs, Business Analysts and junior consultants, and also created reusable playbooks and product-management practices to make discovery, requirements and release planning more consistent across teams. Performance expectations would be clear: ownership, quality of judgment, customer understanding, delivery reliability, cross-functional trust and measurable outcomes. That fits well with Optum's current Lead-level expectations, which explicitly call for clear goals, regular feedback, career development and a culture of ownership, accountability and customer impact. And I like that Optum's broader values include both Relationships and Performance, because good PM leadership requires both.

### Q5.1.5 [Conceptual] What do you look for when hiring senior Product Managers, and how would you build a strong product culture in a large regulated organization like Optum?
*Source: Top 70 document, question 75.*
When I hire a senior Product Manager, I look for judgment before I look for framework knowledge. Most experienced PMs can explain RICE, write a PRD and run a sprint review. What I want to know is whether they can sit with an ambiguous problem, identify what actually matters, challenge a customer respectfully, have a credible conversation with an architect and make a decision when the data is incomplete. I look for five things: customer curiosity, structured thinking, ownership, technical curiosity and influence. At a senior level I would add self-awareness, because the PM has to know when to push hard and when somebody else in the room knows more than they do. In interviews, I prefer real scenarios. I might give the candidate a situation where Business wants a customer feature, Engineering says the architecture is unsafe and Compliance has concerns, then watch how they frame the problem and ask questions. For building culture, I don't think posters or principles are enough. The culture is created by the operating system. I would make customer evidence part of roadmap discussions, require clear success metrics before large investments, expect PMs to understand the technical implications of their decisions and make post-launch learning as important as launch itself. I would build regular product reviews where PMs challenge each other's thinking, create reusable playbooks so the basic craft is consistent, and use one-on-ones for career development rather than project status. I've already used pieces of that approach through mentoring, onboarding, recruitment and internal product-management playbooks, and the broader teams I worked with improved delivery efficiency by around 25%, engineering productivity by 40% and defect-resolution time by 25%. I also think the culture should reflect the environment. In Optum, Integrity, Relationships, Innovation and Performance are especially relevant to Product because we're making decisions in a regulated healthcare business where speed matters but trust matters just as much. The culture I would want is one where PMs have real ownership, challenge each other openly, use evidence, respect domain and technical expertise, and never confuse shipping more with creating more value.

### Q5.1.6 [Experience] Discuss your experience with mentoring and developing junior members of the product team.
*Source: Top 70 document, question 103.*
I've increasingly moved from being the person who solves the product problem to being the person who helps other PMs solve it well. My mentoring tends to focus on judgment rather than templates. I help people learn how to structure a customer conversation, distinguish a feature request from a problem, challenge an engineering estimate constructively and explain a roadmap decision to an executive. I usually give somebody ownership of a real problem and coach around it rather than reviewing every task. In broader teams where we strengthened product discipline, backlog quality and delivery ownership, we saw about 25% better delivery efficiency, alongside improvements in engineering productivity and defect resolution. What I enjoy most is when a PM starts coming to me not with 'what should I do?' but with 'here are the options, here is the evidence, and this is my recommendation.' That is when I know they are developing. I also deliberately expose PMs to customer and engineering conversations early, because product judgment develops through repeated real decisions, not training slides.

### Q5.1.7 [Behavioral] How do you handle feedback, both positive and constructive, from your team members?
*Source: Top 70 document, question 104.*
I try to treat feedback as information about how my leadership is landing, not as a verdict on whether I'm right or wrong. Positive feedback is useful because it tells me what behavior I should repeat, but constructive feedback is often more valuable because it identifies something I cannot see myself. One behavior I've deliberately changed over time is my tendency to go too deep into an important problem when the team is under pressure. Earlier in my career, that helped because I could close gaps quickly. As my scope grew, I realized that stepping in too often could reduce ownership for PMs and Engineering leads. So I've become much more deliberate about asking whether the team needs my answer or simply needs clarity and support. I now use one-on-ones, retrospectives and post-project reviews to actively ask what I could have done differently. The result is that I can remain technically engaged without becoming the decision bottleneck. I think a senior leader should be confident enough to make hard calls and secure enough to change their behavior when the team gives them better information.

## A5.2 Stakeholders, influence and communication

### Q5.2.1 [Behavioral] Tell me about a time you had to align several powerful stakeholders who wanted conflicting things from the same roadmap.
*Source: Top 70 document, questions 115 and 67 (two story options for the same question).*
**Story option A: grocery supply-chain platform**
One example was the large grocery-retail supply-chain platform I worked on at Publicis Sapient. It connected forecasting, ordering, replenishment, ranging, stock, promotions and several other workflows across a large supplier ecosystem. The difficulty was that every senior stakeholder had a legitimate reason why their area should come first. Supply-chain operations wanted automation around ordering. Commercial teams cared about supplier adoption. Technology teams were concerned about integrations and legacy-system dependencies. Leadership wanted measurable savings quickly. With 19 connected systems, simply prioritizing whoever had the most senior sponsor would have created a very fragmented roadmap. I started by getting everyone to agree on a common set of outcomes rather than a common list of features. We focused on ordering cost, manual effort, stock availability, supplier engagement and the legacy complexity we could eliminate. Then we mapped each proposed capability against those outcomes and made dependencies visible. That changed the discussion because a stakeholder could still argue strongly for their requirement, but everyone could see what moving that requirement forward meant for another outcome or dependency. I also sequenced the roadmap so we delivered early operational value while gradually replacing the most expensive legacy processes. Over time, the platform delivered £8.4 million in ordering savings, more than 680 FTE-hours saved each week, 28% higher stock availability and 50% higher supplier engagement across more than 10,000 active users. What I learned is that alignment doesn't mean everybody gets their first choice. It means people understand the decision logic, see that their concern has been considered and can support the final direction even when their item is not first on the roadmap.
**Story option B: platform modernization**
A good example comes from platform modernization at o9 because the stakeholders were looking at the same roadmap through very different lenses. Enterprise customers wanted more capabilities quickly, commercial teams naturally cared about commitments and competitive differentiation, Engineering and Architecture were increasingly concerned about scalability and maintainability, and Operations was dealing with the consequences of reliability issues. All of those priorities were legitimate, but we couldn't do everything at once. My role was to stop the conversation becoming 'features versus technology.' I reframed the roadmap around a few measurable outcomes: customer value, scalability, reliability, implementation effort and cost. We quantified where the existing architecture was becoming a constraint, including concurrency limits, production downtime and repeated engineering effort. Then instead of proposing a long modernization freeze, we sequenced the roadmap so customer delivery continued while the highest-risk API and infrastructure components moved toward cloud-native services. That gave Business visible progress while Engineering could see that the structural problems were being addressed rather than deferred indefinitely. The result was substantial: we reduced production downtime by around 30%, moved scalability from roughly 40 concurrent users to more than 1,000, and generated about $900K in annual infrastructure savings. What I learned from that is that senior stakeholders rarely align because somebody gives a better presentation. They align when everyone can see the same facts and understand what each trade-off costs. My job as the Product Leader is to make those trade-offs explicit and then recommend the path that creates the strongest overall business outcome.

*Source: Top 70 document, question 68 and 116. Questions 68 and 116 describe the same story; the more detailed answer is used.*
A strong example is the B2B commerce transformation I led for a construction-materials client at Publicis Sapient. It involved multiple Product Managers, more than 25 engineers and QA professionals, UX teams and several client-side business stakeholders. Most of those people did not report to me, so formal authority was never really the lever I had. We also had an aggressive sixteen-week window and a large amount of scope across ordering, returns, quotes, recommendations, notifications, templates and customer and associate dashboards. What I focused on was creating enough clarity that teams could move without constantly waiting for escalation. We clarified ownership by journey, established a single prioritized backlog, made dependencies visible and spent time with Engineering explaining the business intent behind the requirements rather than just handing over tickets. With the client, I made sure open decisions and changes were documented so we didn't keep reopening the same debate. When there was a cross-team issue, I tried to resolve it through customer value and delivery risk rather than hierarchy. ### Q5.2.3 [Behavioral] Tell me about a time you had to tell a senior executive or major customer "no." How did you protect the relationship while defending the product strategy?
*Source: Top 70 document, question 70 and 121. Questions 70 and 121 describe the same story; the more detailed answer is used.*
I've had versions of this conversation many times in enterprise SaaS because large customers understandably want the product to behave exactly like their organization. The difficult part is that saying yes to every request can make one customer happy today while making the platform much harder to maintain for everyone tomorrow. My approach has been to avoid starting with 'no.' I start with the outcome they are trying to achieve. In our supply-chain platform, we repeatedly saw customer-specific workflow requirements that could easily have turned into separate implementations. Rather than implementing each one as unique behavior, I worked with the teams to identify the common pattern and move that flexibility into configurable workflows and reusable templates. So the conversation with customers was effectively, 'I understand the outcome you need. We may not implement it exactly in the bespoke way you've described, but we can give you the business outcome through a configuration that will also remain upgrade-safe and supportable.' That approach helped us turn repeated customer requirements into scalable product capabilities and contributed to a 75% reduction in onboarding time and about a 40% improvement in customer satisfaction. I think that is the right way to say no at a senior level. I'm not defending the roadmap because Product knows better than the customer. I'm protecting both the customer's long-term experience and the health of the platform. And when I do need to say a firm no, I try to bring an alternative with it. A clear 'here's what we can do and why I believe it solves your underlying problem better' usually protects the relationship much more effectively than either blindly agreeing or simply rejecting the request.

### Q5.2.4 [Behavioral] Tell me about a difficult conflict between Product, Engineering, Operations, or Compliance that you personally resolved.
*Source: Top 70 document, question 73.*
One useful example comes from a GenAI product in pharmaceutical content creation because there was a very natural tension between speed and compliance. From a Product and business perspective, the opportunity was significant. Generative AI could dramatically reduce the time and cost required to create personalized marketing and medical content. But the compliance and medical-review teams had a very legitimate concern: a fast system that generated an unapproved claim or inappropriate content would create far more risk than value. Engineering could technically make generation increasingly flexible, but Compliance wanted tighter controls, and if we simply optimized for either side we would have ended up with either an unsafe product or a product so constrained that users saw no benefit. My approach was to make compliance part of the product architecture rather than a final approval gate. We anchored generation to approved content and claims, brought Medical, Legal and Regulatory reviewers into the workflow, added guardrails and model-quality checks, and used iterative UAT rather than waiting until the end to discover whether the output was acceptable. We still preserved a fast MVP cadence, delivering the roadmap across 16 weeks, eight sprints and three build-test cycles. The result was about 75% faster end-to-end content creation, 35-45% lower cost for selected content activities, approximately 70% useful generated content during validation, and 100% positive experience feedback in the documented UAT. The important lesson for me was that Product shouldn't mediate these conflicts by asking everyone to compromise equally. You need to redesign the solution so the constraints become part of the product. In a regulated organization like Optum, I would approach Compliance the same way. Bring them into the architecture and workflow early enough that governance enables scale rather than arriving late and blocking it.

### Q5.2.5 [Behavioral] Tell me about a complex initiative involving geographically distributed teams. What did you do to prevent communication and ownership gaps?
*Source: Top 70 document, question 74 and 123. Questions 74 and 123 describe the same story; the more detailed answer is used.*
The B2B construction-materials program at Publicis Sapient is probably the best example. We were working with a U.S. client while much of the Product, Engineering and QA delivery organization was in India. We had multiple product areas moving simultaneously and only a limited daily overlap window. With that structure, you can lose an entire day every time somebody says, 'I'm waiting for the U.S. team to answer this.' So I designed the operating model around asynchronous clarity. We kept requirements, acceptance criteria, dependencies and open decisions documented, and every major decision had a clear owner. I used the India-U.S. overlap for decisions and ambiguity, not status reporting. Before an important client meeting, we would circulate the context and options so the meeting could end with a decision rather than another round of analysis. I also encouraged teams in India to make decisions within defined boundaries instead of waiting for approval across time zones. For cross-team dependencies, we surfaced them early enough that one team was not discovering at the end of its day that another team needed something before it could continue. That operating model helped the team deliver nine major features in sixteen weeks, and the client confidence created by that delivery helped extend the relationship into further phases. We also received multiple recognitions for customer impact. What I learned is that global delivery doesn't become effective by adding more meetings. It becomes effective when the context, decision rights and ownership are clear enough that the product continues moving while half the organization is offline.

### Q5.2.6 [Experience] How do you communicate with cross-functional teams, including engineering, marketing, and sales?
*Source: Top 70 document, question 93.*
I change the language, but I try not to change the underlying truth. One good example was the AI-powered personalization platform I worked on for a large retailer. Engineering needed to understand the data signals, service boundaries and recommendation behavior. Marketing cared about audiences, promotions and content effectiveness. Commercial stakeholders cared about conversion and revenue. If I had taken the same 40-slide deck into every conversation, I would have lost at least two of those groups. So I created a common product narrative around the customer problem and success measures, then translated it differently for each function. With Engineering we went deeper into behavior, data and dependencies. With Marketing we focused on customer segments and experience. With leadership we talked about adoption, conversion and economics. That alignment helped the product achieve roughly 33% higher digital conversion, with the broader initiative associated with $185M in projected sales and around $45M in estimated profit. I think strong cross-functional communication is less about sending more updates and more about helping each team understand how its decisions affect the same product outcome.

### Q5.2.7 [Behavioral] Explain how you handle conflicting priorities and opinions within the team.
*Source: Top 70 document, question 94.*
I try to turn opinions into hypotheses we can compare. On the o9 platform, for instance, commercial teams had customer-facing priorities, Engineering wanted modernization, and Operations wanted greater stability. There was no version of the roadmap where everybody got everything immediately. Rather than letting the loudest voice win, I brought the discussion back to common outcomes: revenue opportunity, customer impact, platform risk, cost, reuse and delivery readiness. Once we quantified that the current architecture was contributing to downtime, limiting concurrency and increasing cost, modernization stopped looking like an Engineering preference. At the same time, we avoided pausing customer delivery by sequencing the modernization rather than rewriting everything. That balance contributed to a platform that reduced downtime by 30%, saved about $900K annually and continued growing commercially. My principle is that disagreement is healthy when people are disagreeing about real trade-offs. The PM's job is to make those trade-offs explicit enough that the team can make one decision and commit to it.

### Q5.2.8 [Behavioral] Discuss your approach to collaborating with sales teams to gather customer requirements.
*Source: Top 70 document, question 95.*
I like Sales as an input channel, but I don't treat every request that reaches Sales as a requirement. In enterprise SaaS, Sales has valuable information because they hear objections, competitor comparisons and unmet customer needs every day. My job is to separate the requested solution from the underlying problem. On B2B transformation work, I would often hear something like, 'the customer needs this exact dashboard' or 'this workflow must work this way.' Rather than immediately adding the feature, I would join discovery discussions, ask how the customer handles the problem today, how often it occurs, what it costs and whether other customers experience the same issue. When the need generalized, we turned it into a reusable platform capability rather than a bespoke feature. That approach on enterprise planning products helped us reduce onboarding time substantially and improve customer satisfaction. It also protects Sales in the long term because they can confidently sell a scalable product rather than a roadmap full of promises. I want Sales to feel that Product listens carefully, but also that I will challenge the request when the evidence points toward a better solution.

### Q5.2.9 [Behavioral] How do you manage expectations with stakeholders, especially when timelines or features change?
*Source: Top 70 document, question 96.*
I try to make bad news early and specific. One project where that discipline mattered was the global pharma GenAI MVP. We had a 16-week roadmap and several dependencies across data, UI, model integration, MLOps and regulated review. With that many moving parts, pretending every original assumption would survive eight sprints would have been unrealistic. So we maintained a clear view of what was committed for the MVP, what assumptions could still change and what dependencies could affect timing. When something changed, I did not just tell stakeholders, 'there's a delay.' I explained what changed, what the impact was, what options we had and what I recommended. That allowed us to protect the core value proposition while iterating on secondary capabilities through three build-and-test cycles. We still delivered the MVP within the 16-week structure and achieved 75% faster content creation, 35-45% lower cost on selected activities and 100% positive experience feedback in UAT. I've found that stakeholders can handle changes much better than surprises. The trust comes from showing that you understand the impact and have a plan.

### Q5.2.11 [Behavioral] How do you keep executive leadership informed about the progress and challenges of a product?
*Source: Top 70 document, question 98.*
I don't give executives sprint reports. I give them a decision-oriented view of the product. On the optimization platform I manage now, leadership cares about whether the product is growing, whether customers can use it effectively, whether the platform can scale and where investment is needed. So my update would typically cover a handful of things: business outcomes, customer adoption, delivery against the strategic roadmap, platform health, major risks and decisions requiring leadership input. ### Q5.2.12 [Experience] Discuss your experience working with UX/UI designers and incorporating design thinking into product development.
*Source: Top 70 document, question 99.*
One project where design thinking was central was the digital transformation of an automotive eCommerce ecosystem. Buying a vehicle online is not a single-page optimization problem. Customers move through discovery, configuration, pricing, lead generation, dealer interaction and test drive before purchase. So the team spent time understanding where users hesitated and what information they needed at each step rather than starting with screen designs. I worked closely with UX and business teams to translate those customer journeys into product priorities, then we iterated the digital experience around those moments. The result was a 27% increase in conversion, 58% reduction in customer decision-making time and 40% increase in test drives. I use design thinking particularly when the customer problem is poorly understood. I want direct observation or interviews, journey mapping, prototypes and usability feedback before Engineering commits to expensive implementation. The designer owns craft, but the PM has to make sure the problem being designed is strategically worth solving.

### Q5.2.13 [Behavioral] How do you handle customer escalations and communicate resolutions to the client?
*Source: Top 70 document, question 100.*
My first rule during an escalation is to separate urgency from emotion. I had a recurring version of this problem in enterprise platform support at o9, where complex bugs could take too long to validate, reproduce and route to the right team. Customers experience that delay as lack of ownership, even when Engineering is actively investigating. We addressed it both operationally and as a product problem. I introduced clearer triage and ownership, and we later deployed an AI agent that automated bug validation, duplicate detection, work-item enrichment and correlation with historical issues. That reduced Mean Time to Activate from days to hours and Mean Time to Resolve from 5.5 days to 1.5 days. During an individual escalation, I communicate in three layers: what we know, what we don't yet know and what happens next. I avoid giving speculative ETAs just to reduce pressure. Once the incident is resolved, I also explain what we are changing so the same class of problem is less likely to recur. That last part matters because customers remember whether you fixed the issue, but they build trust when they see that you learned from it.

### Q5.2.14 [Behavioral] Discuss how you would handle a situation where there's a misalignment between customer expectations and the delivered product.
*Source: Top 70 document, question 110.*
I would start by understanding whether the gap is a product failure, a requirements failure or an expectation-management failure. Those require different fixes. I had a similar situation in enterprise product work where customers often expressed requirements in terms of how their current process worked, while the product was designed around a more reusable platform model. When the delivered experience did not match the customer's mental model exactly, the natural reaction was that something was missing. Instead of immediately customizing the product, I would go back to the business outcome and walk through the gap with the customer. Is the required outcome genuinely impossible, or is the workflow simply different from what they expected? Where we saw repeated patterns across customers, we productized them as configurable capabilities rather than one-off changes. That approach contributed to significantly faster onboarding and higher customer satisfaction. If we truly failed to deliver something we had committed to, I would own that clearly, agree on remediation and communicate timing. The key is not becoming defensive. The customer cares whether their problem gets solved, not whether the original requirements document proves Product was technically correct.

## A5.3 Judgment, delivery under pressure and resilience

### Q5.3.1 [Behavioral] Share an example of a time when you successfully led a team through a challenging project.
*Source: Top 70 document, question 101.*
One project that tested leadership more than almost any other was the B2B construction-materials transformation at Publicis Sapient. We had a large cross-functional team, multiple product areas and a delivery expectation of only sixteen weeks. The challenge was not that the team lacked skill. It was that we could easily lose time through dependencies and repeated decision-making. I spent the first part of the engagement creating very clear ownership around journeys, prioritizing what actually needed to ship and making open decisions visible. We worked in short increments, kept the client close to the product and handled risks before they became sprint-ending surprises. I also tried to protect the team from unnecessary churn by separating genuine requirement changes from stakeholder preferences. We delivered nine significant features in sixteen weeks, including ordering, returns, quotes, recommendations, notifications and dashboards. The program strengthened the client relationship and led to additional phases, and the work received multiple internal recognitions for customer impact. The leadership lesson for me was that under pressure, teams don't need the Product Leader to make every decision. They need clarity, quick decisions when escalation is necessary, and confidence that priorities will not change every forty-eight hours.

### Q5.3.2 [Behavioral] How do you handle stress and tight deadlines in a fast-paced startup environment?
*Source: Top 70 document, question 102.*
I tend to respond to pressure by increasing structure rather than increasing activity. At Ninjacart, the environment moved very quickly and product decisions were closely tied to real-world supply, delivery and unit economics. When we launched the auctioneer marketplace model, we had to validate whether the operating model itself would work, not just whether users liked the app. There was a temptation to build a very broad solution because every operational stakeholder had a requirement. Instead, I kept the first version tightly focused on the transaction and fulfilment model we needed to prove. I used daily operational feedback, field data and short decision loops rather than trying to create certainty upfront. That allowed the MVP to achieve 100% service levels at profitable last-mile costs while reducing quality rejects by 32%. Personally, I manage stress by separating what must be decided today from what can wait, making ownership explicit and communicating risks early. Busy teams often feel stressed because everything appears equally urgent. A Product Leader adds value by deciding what actually deserves urgency.

### Q5.3.3 [Behavioral] If the development team encounters a major technical challenge, how would you handle it?
*Source: Top 70 document, question 105.*
I would first make sure we understand the problem before trying to manage the schedule around it. On the o9 optimization platform, for example, some performance and scalability issues were structural rather than something that could be fixed by adding more sprint capacity. I would bring together the relevant Engineering and Architecture leads, clarify the customer or business impact, identify what assumptions have changed and ask for a small number of technically credible options. Then Product's job is to help evaluate those options through the business lens. What does each option do to scope, timing, reliability, future scalability and cost? In our case, moving selected components toward cloud-native architecture and improving solver efficiency ultimately produced a 65% runtime reduction, 35% lower infrastructure cost, 30% less downtime and roughly $900K in savings. The key is not to pressure Engineering to 'find a workaround' before understanding whether the workaround creates a much bigger problem later. I would keep stakeholders informed with options and impact, give Engineering room to solve the technical problem, and make sure the resulting decision is reflected honestly in the roadmap.

### Q5.3.4 [Technical] How would you prioritize features when faced with limited resources and tight deadlines?
*Source: Top 70 document, question 106.*
**Clarifying questions:** What is the fixed date and why? What is the smallest coherent customer outcome? Which features depend on others? What capacity is truly available?

**Assumptions:** 12 weeks, two squads, 20 candidate features, a business expectation of "everything".

**Framework:** minimum coherent outcome, dependency-aware sequencing, value versus effort.

**Answer (spoken):**
I would reduce the problem to the smallest set of capabilities that creates a complete customer outcome. During the building-materials distributor transformation, we had far more ideas than we could reasonably deliver in the sixteen-week window. Instead of distributing capacity thinly across every request, we prioritized the critical customer journey first, then the capabilities that either unlocked multiple later features or solved the highest-value operational problems. We also looked at dependencies so we did not prioritize something attractive that could not actually ship without three unfinished foundations. That approach let us deliver nine significant features in sixteen weeks rather than twenty half-finished capabilities. In a constrained environment I generally use business value, customer reach, urgency, strategic fit, risk, confidence and engineering effort, but I also ask one simple question: if this feature disappears from the release, does the customer still get a coherent outcome? That keeps the product from becoming a collection of individually valuable but collectively incomplete pieces.
**Recommendation:** commit to a complete core journey, defer the rest openly, and review capacity weekly.

### Q5.3.5 [Behavioral] If a key team member resigns amid a project, how would you mitigate the impact on the product roadmap?
*Source: Top 70 document, question 109.*
I haven't had a resume-documented example where a single resignation created a major roadmap crisis, so I would answer this as a scenario rather than inventing one. My first step would be to understand where the actual dependency sits. Is the person holding unique technical knowledge, customer context, decision authority or simply a large amount of delivery capacity? Those are different risks. I would immediately identify the highest-risk commitments and create a knowledge-transfer plan while the person is still available if possible. Then I would redistribute ownership based on capability, not evenly across the team. If there is a genuine capacity gap, I would revisit scope and sequencing with stakeholders rather than quietly asking the remaining team to absorb everything. I have led large distributed programs where clear documentation, decision logs and shared ownership were important precisely because relying on one person is dangerous. In the sixteen-week B2B delivery, that operating discipline helped us ship nine major features despite complex team dependencies. The broader lesson is that the best mitigation starts before someone resigns. Critical architecture, customer rationale and operational knowledge should not live only in one person's head.

### Q5.3.6 [Behavioral] Tell me about the most serious production or customer-impacting crisis you have led through. How did you behave under pressure?
*Source: Top 70 document, question 111 and 120. Questions 111 and 120 describe the same story; the more detailed answer is used.*
One customer-impacting problem that stands out from my current role was the amount of time complex production defects could remain in triage before Engineering had enough context to act. For a large enterprise customer, that delay can become almost as damaging as the underlying defect because users see the issue sitting unresolved while Support, Product and Engineering try to determine ownership. At one point, Mean Time to Resolve for these issues was around 5.5 days, and activation itself could take days because teams were manually validating the issue, checking for duplicates and searching historical work items. During escalations, my first priority was always to separate immediate containment from root-cause work. I made sure there was one clear owner, that the customer received factual updates rather than speculative ETAs, and that Engineering could focus on investigation rather than answering multiple parallel escalations. But I also felt that becoming better at manually handling incidents was not enough. We looked at the repeated work inside triage and turned that into a product opportunity. We built an AI agent that automated bug validation, duplicate detection, work-item enrichment and correlation against historical issues. That reduced Mean Time to Activate from days to hours and Mean Time to Resolve from 5.5 days to 1.5 days. The broader delivery impact was recognized through a Quarterly Business Milestone Award. The main lesson for me was that during a crisis, calm communication and ownership matter, but after the crisis a Product Leader should ask a harder question: why did the organization need heroics in the first place, and what can we change in the product or operating model so the next incident is easier to diagnose and resolve?

### Q5.3.7 [Behavioral] Describe an important product decision you had to make with incomplete data and significant uncertainty.
*Source: Top 70 document, question 118.*
Ninjacart was a good environment for this because you could not wait for perfect data. One decision I was involved in was launching a new marketplace model that allowed auctioneers and wholesalers to sell directly to fruit and vegetable retailers through the app. We knew the existing model was not ideal for every participant in the ecosystem, but we did not have years of historical data telling us exactly how a new auctioneer-led model would behave. There were real uncertainties around supply reliability, pricing, quality rejection and whether last-mile economics would still work. The wrong way to handle that would have been to spend months trying to model every scenario. Instead, we defined the assumptions that actually had to be true for the idea to work. Could auctioneers reliably supply the assortment? Could we maintain service levels? Could the unit economics support profitable last-mile fulfilment? Could quality be controlled? Then we built the smallest end-to-end version that allowed us to test those assumptions in the real operation. We monitored service, quality and delivery economics closely and adjusted the workflow as we learned. The model ultimately achieved 100% service levels at profitable last-mile cost while reducing quality rejects by 32%. What that taught me is that uncertainty doesn't mean making an unstructured bet. You identify the assumptions with the highest consequence, design the product or experiment to learn about them quickly, and make the next investment only when the evidence improves. I use that approach quite often now, especially with AI products where the technology and user behavior can both be uncertain at the beginning.

### Q5.3.8 [Behavioral] Tell me about a product launch or commitment you missed. What caused it, how did you communicate it, and what did you change afterward?
*Source: Top 70 document, question 119.*
One example was during the B2B commerce transformation I was leading for a large construction-materials client at Publicis Sapient. We had committed to an aggressive roadmap with multiple capabilities going live within a sixteen-week window. Early in the program, I made a commitment on one of the intermediate feature milestones that turned out to be too optimistic.
The mistake was that I had sized the customer-facing workflow reasonably well, but I had underestimated the complexity underneath it. The feature depended on business rules and data coming from multiple legacy systems, and we discovered during development that some of those rules behaved differently depending on the source transaction. By the time we had a complete technical picture, it was clear that forcing the original date would either mean cutting important scenarios or accepting quality risk. We missed that intermediate commitment by about one sprint.
I owned that with the client. I didn't present it as Engineering missing an estimate. I explained that I had committed before we had sufficiently de-risked the integration dependencies. I showed exactly what we had learned, what the remaining risk was, and gave them two choices: ship a narrower workflow on the original date or move the complete capability and protect quality. My recommendation was to move it, and I explained why.
The more important part was what I changed afterward. For the remaining roadmap, we introduced earlier technical spikes for integration-heavy features, mapped external dependencies before committing sprint dates, strengthened Definition of Ready, and brought Engineering and QA into requirement shaping sooner. We also separated the committed scope from stretch scope much more clearly so the customer knew what was genuinely predictable.
That adjustment worked. The overall program still delivered nine major capabilities in sixteen weeks, across ordering, returns, quotes, recommendations, notifications and dashboards, and the strength of the delivery helped us build enough client confidence to continue into subsequent phases. The engagement also received multiple internal recognitions for customer impact.
The lesson for me was very specific: I had treated a feature estimate as mostly a product-scope question when it was actually a system-dependency question. Since then, before I commit to a date on a technically complex feature, I ask three things much earlier: what systems sit underneath this, what assumptions have not yet been validated, and what could make a two-week feature become a six-week integration problem.
So I'm comfortable discussing that miss because it changed how I make commitments. I became much more rigorous about separating confidence from optimism.

### Q5.3.9 [Behavioral] Give me an example of an ambiguous problem where nobody clearly owned the decision. How did you create clarity and move the organization forward?
*Source: Top 70 document, question 124.*
One example from o9 was the product-defect lifecycle. A customer would report an issue, but the path from that report to an actionable Engineering work item crossed Support, Product and Engineering. Support knew the customer context, Engineering knew the code, Product knew the intended behavior, but no single function really owned the quality of that end-to-end handoff. The consequence was predictable: issues could sit in validation for too long, duplicate defects could be created, historical context was hard to find and Mean Time to Resolve was around 5.5 days. Instead of trying to decide which department should 'own' the entire problem, I reframed it as a product workflow. We mapped what information was needed at each point, identified the repeated manual decisions and created clearer responsibility for each stage. Then we took it further and built an AI agent that could validate bugs, identify duplicates, enrich work items and find related historical issues before Engineering picked them up. That changed the process from a series of team handoffs into a more structured lifecycle. Mean Time to Activate dropped from days to hours, and Mean Time to Resolve came down from 5.5 days to 1.5 days. The bigger lesson was that ambiguity often persists because organizations argue about who owns a problem that actually crosses several teams. My approach is to first define who owns the outcome, then make the individual decision rights and handoffs explicit. Once the outcome has an owner, it becomes much easier to decide which part belongs to Product, Engineering or Operations.

### Q5.3.10 [Behavioral] Tell me about your biggest professional failure or a product decision you would make differently today.
*Source: Top 70 document, question 125.*
One product decision I would approach differently today came from an automotive eCommerce transformation I worked on at Publicis Sapient.
When we started, one of my early assumptions was that the ideal experience should move the customer as far as possible toward a completely digital vehicle purchase. The logic seemed sound. Customers increasingly begin their research online, so if we could remove enough friction, simplify configuration and pricing, and allow more of the purchase journey to happen digitally, conversion should improve.
The mistake was that I was thinking too much like a digital-product person and not enough like the customer making a very high-consideration purchase.
As we got deeper into customer behavior and journey data, it became clear that many customers didn't actually want digital to replace the physical experience. They wanted digital to make them more confident before entering it. The dealer interaction and particularly the test drive were not necessarily friction that should be designed away. For many customers they were important confidence-building moments.
That forced me to change my original product framing. Instead of treating success as 'how much of the purchase can we move online?', we started thinking about the journey as one connected experience. Digital should help customers research, compare, configure and narrow their options quickly, and then make the transition into high-value offline actions such as dealer engagement and test drives almost effortless.
We changed the roadmap accordingly. We put more emphasis on reducing decision complexity, carrying customer context across the journey, improving calls to action and making the transition from digital consideration to physical experience much cleaner.
The result was a 27% increase in conversion, a 58% reduction in customer decision-making time and a 40% increase in test drives.
Publicis Sapient's broader automotive work follows a similar principle today, using digital experiences and AI to convert online intent into real-world test drives rather than assuming digital should replace the physical vehicle-buying journey.
What I consider the failure was my initial framing. I jumped too quickly from 'customers are moving online' to 'therefore the goal is maximum digital completion.' Those are not the same thing.
That experience changed the questions I ask in product discovery. I now spend more time asking: What job is the customer actually hiring the digital product to do? Which parts of the existing journey are genuinely friction, and which parts create confidence or trust?
It made me a better Product Manager because I became much less attached to maximizing a channel or a feature and much more focused on optimizing the complete customer outcome.

### Q5.3.12 [Behavioral] Describe a situation where security, compliance or regulatory requirements conflicted with customer experience or business goals. How did you make the trade-off?
*Source: Top 70 document, question 127.*
A strong example was the GenAI marketing and medical-content platform I worked on for a global pharma company. The business opportunity was very clear. Teams wanted to generate personalized content much faster across email, banners, social media and other channels. Generative AI could create huge productivity gains, but in pharma you cannot optimize purely for speed because medical claims, approved content and Medical-Legal-Regulatory review matter. If we gave users a completely open generative experience, it might be more flexible but could create unacceptable compliance risk. If we made every generated output go through a slow manual process, we would remove much of the value of the product. So instead of treating Compliance as a gate at the end, we built it into the workflow. We grounded generation in approved assets and claims, incorporated MLR reviewers, added model-quality checks and guardrails, and used iterative UAT to understand where the AI could be trusted and where human review remained necessary. We also designed the architecture around enterprise services such as Azure OpenAI, vector databases and controlled content repositories rather than sending information through unmanaged tools. The outcome showed that the trade-off worked. End-to-end content creation became roughly 75% faster, selected content activities saw 35-45% lower cost, around 70% of generated content was rated useful during validation, and the documented UAT produced 100% positive experience feedback. That shaped my view of regulated products. I don't see compliance as something that competes with user experience. Good Product Management asks how we can design the controls into the experience so the user gets speed and simplicity while the organization retains the trust, evidence and accountability it needs. That is particularly relevant at Optum, where current technical-product roles explicitly combine AI innovation with Security, Compliance and governance responsibilities.
