### Q6.1.3 [Experience] What would your three-year product vision be for these three interconnected platform pillars?
*Source: Top 70 document, question 130.*
**Situation**
Ford's three pillars, Data Ingestion, the EDP Console, and Vision Intelligence, solve different parts of the same enterprise problem.
The ingestion platform brings structured and unstructured data into the ecosystem. The EDP Console helps users discover, understand, access, govern, and manage that data. Vision Intelligence uses the data to improve manufacturing, diagnosis, quality, recall identification, and Field Service Action processes.
The risk would be to develop them as three independent roadmaps. That could create duplicated capabilities, inconsistent metadata, fragmented user journeys, and AI models that are difficult to scale.
**Task**
My three-year vision would be to build a connected data-to-decision platform in which Ford teams can reliably ingest data, understand and govern it through a unified console, and convert it into production-grade intelligence.
The vision would be:
Any authorized Ford team should be able to bring data into the platform, understand and trust it, use it responsibly, and operationalize intelligence from it with substantially less manual effort.
I would organize the roadmap around three stages: foundation, integration, and intelligence at scale.
**Action**
Year 1: Establish the foundation and prove value
The first year would focus on product clarity, platform reliability, common standards, and a small number of high-value use cases.
For Data Ingestion, I would:
- Define the supported source and format strategy.
- Standardize onboarding for Oracle, SQL Server, PostgreSQL, and DB2.
- Establish reusable patterns for video, audio, PDF, and presentation ingestion.
- Embed metadata capture, lineage, classification, quality checks, access controls, and observability into ingestion.
- Define service-level objectives for reliability, throughput, latency, and recovery.
For the EDP Console, I would:
- Identify the core user personas and top journeys.
- Create a unified navigation and experience model.
- Prioritize foundational journeys such as finding data, evaluating trust, requesting access, monitoring quality, and understanding usage.
- Establish baseline adoption, task-completion, satisfaction, and system-health metrics.
For Vision Intelligence, I would:
- Select two or three high-value use cases using criteria such as safety impact, recall reduction potential, data readiness, feasibility, and workflow adoption.
- Establish model governance and human-review requirements.
- Deliver initial production pilots rather than disconnected proofs of concept.
Year 2: Integrate and scale
The second year would move from foundational capabilities to reusable enterprise services.
Data Ingestion would become more self-service through templates, automated validation, schema-change handling, policy enforcement, and reusable unstructured-data processing components.
The EDP Console would become the orchestration layer across Data Catalog, Data Quality, Metadata Management, Semantic Layer Hub, Ask EDP, Lightspeed, and cost optimization. Users would experience these as connected steps in a journey rather than separate tools.
Vision Intelligence would expand from initial use cases into a reusable platform with shared capabilities for data preparation, labeling, model deployment, monitoring, feedback collection, and retraining.
This year would also introduce stronger platform analytics and FinOps capabilities, enabling product teams to see adoption, quality, performance, storage, compute usage, and cost drivers in one place.
Year 3: Deliver proactive intelligence
By the third year, the platform should move from enabling analysis to proactively supporting decisions.
The ingestion layer would support policy-aware, event-driven data flows and automatically apply the appropriate metadata, quality, retention, and governance controls.
The EDP Console would become more personalized and intelligent. It could recommend trusted datasets, flag quality or cost issues, summarize lineage, surface relevant semantic definitions, and help users complete common data tasks through conversational experiences such as Ask EDP.
Vision Intelligence would support production-scale anomaly detection, automated quality checks, faster diagnosis, and more efficient identification of recall and FSA populations. Models would be continuously monitored, and human feedback would flow back into model improvement.
**Result**
At the end of three years, I would expect measurable outcomes in four areas:
- Speed: Reduce source onboarding and data-discovery time by [X%].
- Adoption: Increase active platform usage and self-service completion by [Y%].
- Efficiency: Reduce manual quality, diagnosis, and recall-identification effort by [Z%].
- Trust and economics: Improve data-quality compliance while reducing avoidable compute and storage costs.
The key result would not simply be three successful products. It would be one connected ecosystem in which ingestion, governance, discovery, cost management, and AI reinforce one another.

### Q6.1.4 [Experience] How would you prioritize investments across Data Ingestion, the EDP Console, and Vision Intelligence when all three have urgent stakeholder demands?
*Source: Top 70 document, question 131.*
**Situation**
In a portfolio like this, every stakeholder can make a credible argument that their need is urgent.
Engineering may need ingestion reliability and scalability. Data consumers may need a simpler console experience. Quality and manufacturing teams may see immediate value in Vision Intelligence. Governance may require stronger privacy and control mechanisms. Leadership may expect visible AI outcomes while also demanding cost discipline.
In my experience, prioritization becomes ineffective when it is based primarily on executive volume, individual escalation, or a single financial estimate.
**Task**
My responsibility would be to create a transparent portfolio framework that balances foundational platform health, business value, risk, user impact, and strategic learning.
I would also make dependencies visible. For example, a Vision Intelligence use case may have substantial value, but it cannot scale if unstructured ingestion, metadata, security, labeling, or monitoring capabilities are missing.
**Action**
I would use a five-part prioritization framework.
1. Business and customer impact
I would assess the expected impact on safety, quality, recall reduction, diagnostic speed, productivity, platform adoption, or cost.
For this role, initiatives connected to vehicle quality, recall identification, or regulatory exposure would receive significant weight because the potential consequences extend beyond ordinary productivity gains.
2. Strategic leverage
I would favor investments that enable multiple products or use cases.
For example, a reusable video-ingestion and metadata capability may support several Vision Intelligence use cases. A unified access-request workflow in the EDP Console may improve every data domain. These investments have greater portfolio leverage than one-off custom solutions.
3. Risk and obligation
Security, privacy, reliability, governance, and regulatory requirements cannot be evaluated only through immediate return on investment.
I would reserve capacity for mandatory work and distinguish between:
- Non-negotiable risk remediation.
- Platform resilience work.
- Strategic growth.
- User-experience improvements.
- Experiments and discovery.
4. Readiness and time to evidence
I would evaluate whether the required data, users, workflows, engineering capacity, and governance approvals are available.
A high-value initiative with very low readiness may begin as a discovery or data-readiness effort rather than receiving a full delivery investment. Conversely, a use case that can demonstrate value quickly may be prioritized to build confidence and generate learning.
5. Measurable outcomes and confidence
Every major investment would need a defined outcome, baseline, target, and evidence plan.
I would use a portfolio scorecard rather than treating all roadmap items as directly comparable. The scorecard could include:
- Safety and quality impact.
- User reach.
- Financial impact.
- Strategic reuse.
- Risk reduction.
- Data and technical readiness.
- Time to value.
- Confidence in the assumptions.
- Cost of delay.
I would then allocate capacity across horizons. As an example:
- Approximately 40% for ingestion foundations, reliability, governance, and shared services.
- Approximately 30% for the EDP Console and user adoption.
- Approximately 20% for high-value Vision Intelligence use cases.
- Approximately 10% for discovery and emerging opportunities.
Those percentages would not be fixed permanently. They would change as platform maturity and evidence evolved.
**Result**
This approach produces three benefits.
First, stakeholders can understand why an initiative was prioritized or deferred. Second, foundational work is connected to visible business outcomes rather than being presented as technical maintenance. Third, Ford can pursue high-value Vision Intelligence use cases without creating fragile, one-off solutions.
I would review the portfolio quarterly and monitor outcomes more frequently. If a Vision Intelligence use case showed strong evidence of recall reduction or diagnostic improvement, I would increase investment. If ingestion reliability became a constraint across multiple domains, I would shift capacity toward the shared foundation.
My goal would be to avoid choosing one pillar over the others. I would prioritize the sequence of investments that creates the most valuable and scalable end-to-end outcome.
