## 7.2 Story 1. Solver modernization and a $5M+ ARR platform

The story I'd lead with is the Linear Programming Solver at o9, which is now a product above $5 million in ARR. It's the optimization engine behind supply, production, deployment and network planning for enterprise customers, and I own its strategy and roadmap, inside a wider portfolio that serves more than 200 enterprise customers. The challenge was a balancing act. Customers wanted to run much larger models and wanted the optimizer to follow their real business policies, and at the same time the platform underneath needed modernization to keep up. My job was to set priorities across customer requests, platform work and longer-term technology bets, and to make sure each one paid off in something we could measure. I spearheaded the modernization work. We reworked parts of the solver architecture, and optimization runtime came down 65 percent, so planners could evaluate significantly larger models and get answers faster. Scalability improved by another 20 to 30 percent. On the customer side, I introduced proportional sourcing constraints and configurable optimization logic, so the models followed how a customer's business really runs, and sourcing accuracy went up 40 percent. I also brought in tenant-level resource management, which cut infrastructure cost by 35 percent, and telemetry-driven monitoring that gave us real-time visibility into performance and convergence across customer environments. Then I looked at what happens when something goes wrong. We built infeasibility diagnosis that cut debugging effort by 60 percent, and I worked with the data science team to test AI-assisted parameter tuning to reduce manual configuration. What I'm most pleased about is that these weren't separate efforts. Faster runtimes, better accuracy, easier debugging and lower cost all showed up in the same product, and it grew past $5 million in ARR. Owning several product portfolios taught me to ask the same thing of every roadmap item: which metric will move, by how much, and how will we know. I would rather have a smaller roadmap where every item has an answer than a long one where nobody can tell what worked. When I plan a roadmap, I try to hold three questions at once: will it make the product faster, will it make the output more accurate, and will users trust it enough to rely on it. Customers ask for features, but they stay because the answers are fast, correct and explainable. That's the approach I'd bring to a payment integrity platform that has to serve several clients and lines of business, where speed, accuracy and trust decide whether anyone uses it.

- Best for: roadmap trade-offs, platform ownership, "tell me about a product you scaled".
- To cut to 90 seconds: keep the runtime, accuracy, cost and ARR results, and drop telemetry and the data science work.
- Evidence to be ready for: all figures are achieved results from your resume; be ready to explain your role versus the team's and the period over which ARR reached $5 million.
- Optional 20-second add-on: what you chose not to build to make room for modernization.

## 7.3 Story 2. Monolith to cloud-native with observability

Our enterprise platform APIs and developer infrastructure were built as a monolith, and we were serving more than 200 enterprise customers on it. It scaled to roughly 40 concurrent users, production issues were frequent, and troubleshooting took too long. I led the product side of moving it to cloud-native services, with monitoring built in, so that global manufacturers could run it at enterprise scale. I worked closely with the architects and engineers, and I brought the customer view into every decision. I ran voice-of-customer sessions with customers and used what they told us to shape where the platform investment went. We rebuilt the APIs and developer infrastructure as cloud-native services. We set up centralized monitoring and telemetry pipelines with anomaly detection and proactive monitoring, plus continuous monitoring that caught execution failures early, so the team could respond to incidents quickly. I moved 13 integration workflows to a next-generation framework and built reusable integration accelerators, which cut implementation effort and made customer deployments more consistent. I also pushed to automate release validation, regression testing and deployment through CI/CD, and release preparation went from several days to a few hours. We supported adoption too. I designed learning programs and training content for more than 3,000 global users. The results were clear. Concurrent users went from about 40 to more than 1,000. Production downtime fell 30 percent, API-related production issues fell 90 percent, and time spent diagnosing and debugging issues dropped 40 percent. The new setup saved roughly $900,000 a year in infrastructure. The way I run platform work is to attach a number to each piece of it. Concurrent users, downtime, issue diagnosis time and release effort each had a baseline, so we could show progress to customers and to leadership. It also made it easier to protect the time the work needed, and it gave the engineers a clear target to aim at. What I take from this is that reliability is a product feature, and customers feel it. Platform work always competes with new features, and customer feedback was what justified the investment, because it pointed to scale, stability and faster delivery. At Optum, where platforms carry claims and audit workloads for large payers and providers, I'd bring the same instinct: build observability and resilience in from the start, tie them to a metric, and show customers and leadership what they gain.

- Best for: architecture trade-offs, technical debt, cloud, DevOps, reliability.
- To cut to 90 seconds: keep users 40 to 1,000+, downtime, the $900K and the CI/CD point.
- Evidence to be ready for: all figures achieved; separate your product role from engineering's technical choices.

## 7.4 Story 3. Making solver decisions explainable

A recurring problem with the solver was trust. When a model was infeasible, planners had no quick way to see which constraints were in conflict, and without that they couldn't act on the result with confidence. I owned the roadmap for the solver, so I made it a roadmap priority. The goal was to make the solver's decisions understandable and debuggable. We built an infeasibility diagnosis capability that points to conflicting constraints and optimization bottlenecks, and it cut solver debugging effort by 60 percent. Then I turned to the second half of the problem, which is that a correct answer in mathematical form isn't much use to a business user. I worked on an AI-assisted explanation layer that translates the optimizer's output into business recommendations in plain language. I partnered with the data science team to test it in pilot evaluations, and in those pilots interpretation effort dropped by about 50 percent. To be clear about the status, the debugging result is in production and the explanation result comes from pilots. I like to be precise about that, because trust is the whole point. I also added telemetry-driven monitoring so we had real-time visibility into solver performance and convergence. I also care about who an explanation is for. A planner, a consultant and an executive want different levels of detail, so I treat the explanation as part of the user experience and check it with real users, not only with data scientists. That is also why the debugging capability and the explanation layer are separate pieces: one tells you where the problem is, and the other tells you what the answer means. Taken together, the idea was simple: show the user what the system decided, why it decided it, and where it is struggling. Planners could identify conflicting constraints quickly, and that is what improved their confidence in the tool. This is exactly what I think payment integrity needs. When a claim is flagged or an audit finding is raised, the reviewer and the provider both need to see why, quickly and clearly. If I owned that product, I'd measure review time, how often reviewers agree with the flag, how often findings are overturned on appeal, and how many provider disputes come back. I haven't built that for claims yet, but I've built the same capability for a different kind of decision, and I know how to define the metrics and test the explanations with real users before scaling them.

- Best for: explainability, human-in-the-loop, AI evaluation, "how would you apply AI to audit decisions".
- To cut to 90 seconds: keep the 60 percent and 50 percent results and the pilot label.
- Evidence to be ready for: debugging result achieved; interpretation result from pilots; decide what shipped versus piloted.

## 7.5 Story 4. A production GenAI assistant for scientific product support

For a scientific-instruments client, I delivered one of their first production-grade GenAI assistants. The problem was easy to state. Customers had complex questions about laboratory products, the answers lived in static technical documents, and getting an answer could take hours. The risk was just as clear. These customers were scientists, so a wrong answer would damage trust quickly. My task was to deliver an assistant that was fast and accurate enough for the client's own scientists to approve. I designed a production architecture built on retrieval. It used a vector database, document embeddings, careful preprocessing of the documents, reranking and validation of each response. I also added responsible-AI guardrails and multi-prompt validation, so the assistant checked its answer before showing it. Then I worked directly with the client's scientists to validate accuracy until they were satisfied. The results held up. Query resolution time dropped about 60 times, from hours to seconds. Average retrieval precision was above 95 percent across more than 1,000 scientific documents. And the client's scientists formally approved the assistant before it went live, which for me is the strongest result, because the experts decided it was good enough. The product also changed how the client shares information. Static technical documents became a conversational experience, which made product information easier to reach, and it gave them a reusable AI base for customer engagement and knowledge retrieval across the business. The model we used then was GPT-3.5, and models have moved on since, so I'd revisit that choice today, but the approach of retrieval, validation and expert sign-off still holds. What I would highlight is how we thought about quality. Retrieval precision told us whether the right passages were being found, the validation steps told us whether the answer was supported by them, and the scientists' review told us whether it was actually useful. Looking at those separately made it clear where to improve. In healthcare operations the same pattern applies. Whether the question is about a benefit, a policy or a prior authorization requirement, an assistant should answer from approved sources, show where the answer came from, and earn the trust of the people who review it. That is how I'd design a payer assistant, and I'd bring the same evaluation discipline.

- Best for: RAG, hallucination control, AI evaluation, responsible AI.
- To cut to 90 seconds: keep the 60 times, the 95 percent and the scientist approval.
- Evidence to be ready for: figures achieved; how retrieval precision was measured and by whom.

## 7.6 Story 5. A GenAI content platform for a global pharma company

A global pharma company wanted to create personalized marketing and medical content much faster, across email, banners, social and display channels. The catch was that every piece of content goes through medical, legal and regulatory review, so speed couldn't come at the cost of compliance. I drove the development and scaling of the platform. I started with a 16-week MVP plan across eight sprints and three build-and-test cycles, with the data ingestion, user interface and MLOps foundations in place within the first six to eight weeks. That gave us a base we could extend to more brands, channels and geographies afterward. I helped shape the architecture, which used Azure OpenAI, vector databases, integrations with the content and asset systems, and models that enrich content for generation, translation, image recommendations, claim retrieval and regulatory checks. I built the business case and the value framework, and I made sure the review process lived inside the product. That meant the reviewers, the approved claims, the guardrails and the quality metrics were part of the workflow from the beginning, and we ran repeated user acceptance testing along the way. The results were strong. End-to-end content creation became about 75 percent faster, and cost fell 35 to 45 percent for the content and copy activities we targeted. In user acceptance testing, feedback was 100 percent positive, and about 70 percent of the generated content was rated useful. The business case estimated roughly $34 million a year in content production savings, with a wider potential above $220 million in efficiencies, and I want to be clear that these are estimates. It also meant teams could produce much more content within existing budgets. I also learned a lot about adoption. The reviewers weren't asked to trust a black box. The approved claims sat inside the workflow, so they could check a draft against them quickly, and I think that helped the testing go as well as it did. What this shows is that AI can speed up work in a heavily reviewed workflow when the reviewers are designed in, not added at the end. That's the approach I'd take on any payer or provider workflow where decisions carry regulatory weight, because the review step is part of the product, not an obstacle to it.

- Best for: AI lifecycle, governance, MVP, business case.
- To cut to 90 seconds: keep the 16-week MVP, the 75 percent, the 100 percent UAT and the estimate label.
- Evidence to be ready for: 75 percent and 35 to 45 percent are achieved; $34M and $220M+ are estimates; define your role in the architecture.

## 7.7 Story 6. Cutting onboarding by 75% with configurable templates

Our flagship supply chain planning product was powerful, but implementing it was heavy. Customers took a long time to onboard, planning runs and inventory rebalancing were slow, and the setup was complex. I owned the product, and my goal was to cut time to value and improve the planner experience across industries. I started by running discovery workshops with customers across several industries to understand their planning challenges. Then I redesigned the planning workflows to remove implementation complexity. I worked on the planning engine's execution logic. And I built the organization's first industry-specific supply planning template, for apparel and footwear, which standardized how those implementations start. I also added configurable movement constraints and parameters, so plans reflected real operations without custom code, and I configured advanced capabilities for transportation optimization, deployment planning and inventory balancing that supported complex global supply chains. The numbers were good. Customer onboarding time fell 75 percent. Batch execution time fell 70 percent, and inventory rebalancing runtime fell 85 percent. Planning accuracy rose 22 percent, and customer satisfaction went up 40 percent. Consultant productivity improved as well. A lot of that came from listening. The workshops showed what customers across industries needed from planning, and fixing a pain once in the product helped every customer after that, not only the one in the room. The part I'd stress is the template idea. A template turns what you learned from one customer into a starting point for the next, while still letting each customer configure what is different. In my view that's the heart of a platform that serves many clients and lines of business. It also protects the roadmap, because the team builds one configurable capability and not a custom version for each customer. For a payment integrity product, I'd apply the same thinking: a shared core, templates by line of business, and configuration that lets each client apply its own rules and thresholds without a custom build. And I'd measure it the way I did here, by how long a new client takes to go live, how accurate the results are, and how satisfied the users are.

- Best for: configurable multi-client design, templates, discovery, outcome metrics.
- To cut to 90 seconds: keep the 75 percent, the 85 percent, the template and the satisfaction result.
- Evidence to be ready for: figures achieved; define how onboarding time was measured.

## 7.8 Story 7. A field service case-management platform

For a national roadside assistance provider, I directed improvements to the field operations platform that coordinates dispatch and service across the whole country. The operation had a lot of moving parts: service requests coming in, technicians out in the field, and operations managers trying to see what was happening in real time. The task was to make that coordination work better and give people the visibility to act. I designed intelligent dispatch and incident-management workflows, so requests were assigned and tracked as they moved from intake to resolution. I built configurable case management that covers service requests, technician assignment, route optimization and operational reporting. I set the roadmap priorities around real-time fleet visibility, work-order management and customer communication, which improved operational transparency and service quality. And I added analytics dashboards where operations managers could watch field performance, response times and technician productivity. I worked with operations leaders and customer support teams to find where the workflow slowed down, and I used those conversations to decide what to improve. The key result was that average service response time fell 22 percent, and field resources were used better as well. What I like about that result is how it came about. We didn't add features for their own sake. Each change was aimed at a bottleneck that operations teams had pointed to, and the dashboards let managers see whether the change worked. I also kept each role in mind. Dispatchers, technicians and operations managers use the platform differently, and configurable case management let each of them work from what they needed to see. I mention this one for Optum because the shape of the problem is similar to an audit or recovery platform. Work comes in, it gets triaged and assigned, someone works the case, evidence and outcomes are recorded, and managers need dashboards to see throughput and cycle time. The details are very different in healthcare, and I'd need to learn the audit workflow from the people who run it, so I'd spend my first weeks with auditors and reviewers. But I know how to design configurable case management from intake to outcome, and how to measure it through response time, throughput and productivity. In an audit setting I'd add yield, overturn rate and provider disputes to that list, so we measure whether the work is correct, not only whether it is fast.

- Best for: workflow and case management, dashboards, "how would you design an audit workflow".
- To cut to 90 seconds: keep the intake to resolution flow, the 22 percent and the Optum connection.
- Evidence to be ready for: 22 percent achieved; utilization improvement was not quantified.

## 7.9 Story 8. A digital healthcare experience platform

For a global healthcare organization, I directed a digital transformation focused on how patients register, book appointments and find care. Patients were running into friction at each of those steps, and administrators couldn't easily see where the bottlenecks were. My task was to define the vision for an enterprise healthcare experience platform and make those journeys simpler. I redesigned the journeys to remove friction from registration, appointment management and digital service interactions. I built configurable workflows so the platform could support personalized patient communications and coordinate services across different care channels. I added customer analytics and operational dashboards so administrators could measure engagement and find service bottlenecks themselves. Throughout, I worked with clinicians, operations leaders, UX designers and engineers, because a healthcare experience only works if it respects how care is actually delivered. We also set up experimentation and feedback loops, so we could keep improving the digital services with data after launch, and translate healthcare challenges into product capabilities that could scale. The results were clear. Patient satisfaction went up 30 percent, and successful digital task completion went up 26 percent. I'm proud of this work because the users were patients trying to get care, and the changes made that easier. One thing I would highlight is the role of analytics. By giving administrators dashboards on engagement and bottlenecks, we made improvement a routine for the team and not a one-time project, and the experiments let us check each change against data before we scaled it. I want to be clear about the scope. It was a patient-experience platform, not a claims or clinical system. But it taught me how to work with clinicians and operations teams, how to build for configurability across care channels, and how to measure healthcare outcomes with patient-centered metrics next to operational ones. At Optum, I'd carry those habits into a platform where the users are reviewers, auditors and care teams: start with how the work is really done, measure the experience as well as the output, and keep improving after launch. I'd bring the AI and platform experience I've built since, and I'd make sure the people who use the product have a voice in how it develops.

- Best for: healthcare product experience, working with clinicians, configurable workflows, experimentation.
- To cut to 90 seconds: keep the journeys, the clinician partnership and the 30 and 26 percent results.
- Evidence to be ready for: figures achieved; how satisfaction was measured.

## 7.10 Story 9. GenAI-assisted data engineering for a health insurer

For a large global health insurer, I worked on how AI could speed up the data engineering that supports marketing campaigns. The team was building data pipelines across more than 300 data sources to power customer-targeted campaigns, and delivery effort was high. My task was to make GitHub Copilot a real part of how the team worked, and to do it without lowering quality. I took a lifecycle view, not just code generation. We used AI assistance for requirements, data discovery, mapping, engineering, unit testing, quality engineering and business validation. Beyond code, we extended it to metadata cataloguing, SQL and transformation logic, unit-test scaffolding, boundary-condition tests and test-data generation. I also introduced coding standards for AI-assisted work and preferred implementation patterns, so quality stayed consistent and cloud compute cost went down. The measurable results were these. Sprint efficiency improved 25 to 30 percent and developer productivity rose about 25 percent. We cut the estimated delivery effort from 4,091 hours to 3,082, which is more than 1,000 hours. And the team delivered more than 50 production features across those 300-plus data sources. I treated the gains as a team habit and not a tool rollout. Coding standards, test scaffolding and business validation steps became part of how the team worked, and that is what I would repeat. I also kept testing in view. We automated unit-test scaffolding, boundary-condition tests and test-data generation, which freed time for the harder business rules, while the quality engineering and business validation steps stayed in place. I'd note two things for accuracy. The hours figure is a reduction in estimated effort, and this was marketing data, not claims or payer operations. What carries over to Optum is the method. Treat AI as a change to the whole delivery process, not a tool for one step. Set quality standards for AI-written work. And measure the effect in hours, features and cost so leadership can see it. I would apply that to platform and data teams in a healthcare setting, where the datasets are large, the quality bar is high, and the pressure to deliver more with the same team is real.

- Best for: AI-enabled productivity, SDLC, data pipeline delivery, healthcare data.
- To cut to 90 seconds: keep the lifecycle view, the 25 to 30 percent and the 1,000 hours.
- Evidence to be ready for: efficiency figures achieved; the hours figure is estimated effort; clarify your product role versus the engineers' role.

## 7.11 Story 10. Registration automation and an Agile operating-model change

For a scientific-instruments company's online store, registering as a customer was slow and manual. Existing customers waited about 24 hours and new customers about 48, because the process depended on Sales, master data and the service desk. There were around 12,000 registrations a year, and delivery was slow too: development cycles took two to four weeks and time to market was seven weeks. My task was to fix the registration journey and the way we delivered. I turned account identification, verification and mapping into an automated product, with approval workflows and analytics around it, so fewer people had to touch each registration. At the same time, I changed how we delivered. We moved to MVP-based releases with CI/CD and feature toggles, raised automated test coverage, and set up KPI-driven governance with analytics, SLA tracking, Jira roadmaps and product-oriented squads. The results were large. Registration for existing customers went from 24 hours to under a minute, and new-customer onboarding from 48 hours to about 30 minutes. Development cycles shrank from two to four weeks to one to two, and time to market from seven weeks to under three. Automated test coverage went from 50 percent to over 85 percent, first-time-pass rate rose from 93 to 96 percent, and regression effort dropped from about 70 hours to under five. The projected savings were around 20,000 person-days, and I'd call that a projection, not a result. This work sat in the path to purchase for a portfolio serving more than 40,000 customers, so removing the wait mattered commercially. Looking back, what made it work was that we agreed on the baseline before we changed anything. Registration time, cycle time, test coverage and regression effort all had a starting number, and SLA tracking and Jira roadmaps kept those numbers visible to the team and to stakeholders. What I like about this story is that the product change and the delivery change reinforced each other. A faster registration journey only stays fast if the team can keep shipping improvements safely. For Optum, it shows how I approach any manual, multi-team process: measure the baseline, automate the repetitive part, and improve the way the team ships so the gains last.

- Best for: process improvement with baselines, quality engineering, KPI governance, working with operations teams.
- To cut to 90 seconds: keep the 24 hours to under a minute, 7 weeks to under 3 and test coverage 50 to 85 percent.
- Evidence to be ready for: figures achieved except the 20K person-days projection; how baselines were measured.
